## Unit 3 — Populations

### Section A — MCQ Answers and Explanations

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ001
**Answer: C.** A specialist depends on a narrow set of resources or conditions, so it does best in stable habitats and suffers when those conditions change. The option describing wide tolerance and success in changing habitats is the definition of a generalist, and the other two attribute generalist or r-selected traits to specialists.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ002
**Answer: D.** K-selected species invest heavily in a few offspring, mature late, live long, and breed repeatedly. The first distractor lists the r-selected package, while the other two each mix one K-selected trait with traits that do not belong together (few offspring with no care; very many offspring with extensive care).
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ003
**Answer: B.** Very heavy mortality among the youngest individuals followed by relatively good survival of the few that persist is a Type III curve, which is typical of r-selected species that produce huge numbers of offspring. Pairing Type III with K-selected is the most tempting error, but K-selected species produce few offspring and follow Type I or II.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ004
**Answer: B.** Carrying capacity (K) is set by resources and space and is the largest population the habitat can support over the long term. The first distractor describes biotic potential, and the second confuses K with the point of fastest absolute growth, which occurs near K/2.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ005
**Answer: D.** When resources are abundant, per-capita reproduction stays high and a larger breeding base adds more rabbits each year, so growth accelerates (the early part of a J-shaped curve). Resources are finite, but they are not yet limiting, so growth does not slow until the population nears K.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ006
**Answer: A.** A broad base means many children who will enter their reproductive years, which signals rapid growth. Small older groups do not signal decline here; they reflect high mortality and a young, fast-growing population. A stable population would give a more column-like shape.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ007
**Answer: A.** A TFR of about 2.1 is the replacement level, meaning each generation just replaces itself, so the population is relatively stable. A TFR near 2 is not high enough to cause rapid growth or doubling in 35 years, and it is not low enough to cause a sharp decline.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ008
**Answer: C.** Population size changes through births and immigration (gains) and deaths and emigration (losses). Each distractor pairs one gain with a loss.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ009
**Answer: A.** Stage 1 is the pre-industrial stage: birth rates are high, but disease, famine, and poor sanitation keep death rates high as well, so the population grows very slowly. In Stage 2 death rates fall while birth rates stay high, which produces rapid growth.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ010
**Answer: D.** Overshoot means the population is using resources faster than they are replaced; depletion leads to famine, disease, or conflict and a dieback. Carrying capacity does not increase because the population is large (it often falls after depletion), and competition rises, not falls, during overshoot.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ011
**Answer: C.** A Type I curve stays high through most of the life span and then drops steeply in old age, as in humans in countries with good healthcare. Constant mortality describes Type II, and very high early mortality describes Type III.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ012
**Answer: B.** Density-independent factors such as fires, storms, heat waves, and droughts affect the same proportion of a population regardless of its density. Competition for nest sites, disease transmission, and food depletion all become more severe as density rises, so they are density-dependent.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ013
**Answer: B.** Biotic potential is the highest possible rate of reproduction when resources are unlimited and conditions are ideal. The first distractor is carrying capacity, and the second describes realized (not maximum) reproductive success after environmental resistance has acted.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ014
**Answer: C.** Species 1 is a specialist and Species 2 is a generalist. When habitat changes, generalists are advantaged because they can use alternative resources, whereas specialists lose the single resource they depend on. The prediction that both decline equally ignores the difference in dietary breadth.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ015
**Answer: A.** Biotic potential rises with the number of offspring per event and falls as age at first reproduction increases. Species P combines 400 eggs with maturity at 0.4 years, giving by far the highest maximum reproductive rate. Species S is the most tempting alternative, but its litter is 50 times smaller and it matures 2.5 times later than P.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ016
**Answer: C.** K-selected species have few offspring, mature late, and have low reproductive rates, so losses from an invader are replaced slowly. Many r-selected species rebound quickly, and most invasive species are themselves r-selected. The distractors attribute r-selected traits (fast reproduction, short life span) to the K-selected native.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ017
**Answer: D.** A K-selected mammal with few young and heavy parental investment has high survival through most of its life span, which gives the Type I shape shown by Curve 2. Curve 1 is the Type III pattern of heavy early mortality, produced by species with huge numbers of unprotected young, and Curve 3 is the straight line on a log axis that represents constant mortality (Type II).
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ018
**Answer: A.** A constant probability of death means a constant fraction of the remaining survivors dies each interval, which plots as a straight diagonal line on a log scale (Type II, Curve 3). Curve 2 shows very low early mortality and a collapse late in life, and Curve 1 shows extremely high early mortality followed by slower losses.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ019
**Answer: D.** In logistic growth the number of individuals added per year, rN(1 − N/K), is greatest when the population is about half the carrying capacity. Here K = 600, so the steepest part of the curve is at about 300 sheep, which is point S. Point P has more sheep, but resource limits have already slowed its growth, and point R is essentially at K, where growth is near zero.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ020
**Answer: B.** Deer numbers climbed while browse fell, and the herd peaked when browse was nearly gone; the resulting starvation caused dieback that let browse recover slowly. Lower browse means a lower carrying capacity, not a higher one, and the weather records rule out a density-independent cause.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ021
**Answer: B.** A valid test changes only the independent variable (surface area), keeps starting numbers, nutrients, and light the same, replicates treatments, and tracks the dependent variable (plant count) over time. The other designs vary starting numbers, light, or nutrients along with, or instead of, surface area, so any difference could not be attributed to surface area.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ022
**Answer: C.** All three cultures grew at nearly the same rate early on, but each leveled off at a different population size that matched its glucose supply, showing that the limiting resource sets carrying capacity. The cultures did not reach the same plateau, and constant temperature rules out temperature, not a density-dependent resource limit.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ023
**Answer: C.** Country C has by far the largest share of its population in the youngest age groups, so a large cohort will soon enter its reproductive years and births will greatly exceed deaths. Country D also has a broad base, but its younger groups are much smaller relative to its middle-aged groups, so its growth will be slower. Countries A and B have nearly constant or inverted shapes, indicating stable or declining populations.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ024
**Answer: B.** Country B has a narrow base and its largest groups are middle-aged adults who will move into retirement while fewer young people enter the workforce, so its ratio of retirees to workers will rise the most. Country A is also fairly column-shaped, but it has a larger share of children and a smaller bulge in the middle-aged groups. Country C is very young, so its retiree ratio will stay low.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ025
**Answer: D.** Education for girls and access to family planning are two of the main factors that lower TFR: they delay the age at first birth and give women more control over the number of children. Fertility is strongly influenced by social and policy factors, and the decrease does not require higher infant mortality; infant mortality typically falls as these changes occur.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ026
**Answer: B.** Infant mortality is strongly affected by whether mothers have access to good healthcare and adequate nutrition. A higher TFR and more child labor are characteristics of regions with higher, not lower, infant mortality, and total population size does not determine a death rate per 1,000 births.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ027
**Answer: D.** Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (%) = 70 ÷ 0.7 = 100 years. Multiplying instead of dividing gives 49, using 70 as the answer ignores the rate, and moving the decimal the wrong way gives 10.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ028
**Answer: A.** RNI = CBR − CDR = 17 − 11 = 6 per 1,000 per year. Converting to a percentage: 6 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 0.6% per year. Choosing 6% forgets that 6 per 1,000 is 0.6%, and 28 results from adding the two rates.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ029
**Answer: D.** The percentage infected rose steadily with density (2% to 31%), which is the signature of a density-dependent factor; crowding increases transmission. A density-independent factor would affect the same proportion at every density. The data say nothing about carrying capacity, and the percentages are clearly not equal.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ030
**Answer: A.** This pattern of falling deaths and still-high births is Stage 2 of the demographic transition model, shown here as Stage X, and it produces rapid growth. In Stage W both rates are high and balanced, in Stage Y the birth rate is falling toward the death rate, and in Stage Z both rates are low.

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ031
**Answer: A.** Developing countries typically have higher infant mortality and more children working, which are linked with higher birth rates. Each distractor pairs one correct characteristic with its opposite.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ032
**Answer: C.** Malthusian theory holds that population grows geometrically while food production grows arithmetically, so growth is eventually limited by famine, disease, and conflict. The statement about families choosing smaller sizes describes the demographic transition, and the last two reject the central Malthusian premise.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ033
**Answer: C.** r-selected and K-selected describe the ends of a continuum; many species have traits of both or change reproductive strategy depending on conditions. R-selected species generally have the higher biotic potential and are usually smaller than K-selected species, so the last two statements are reversed or overstated.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ034
**Answer: A.** Carrying capacity is set by the availability of resources, so a sustained increase in forage raises it. Drought lowers it, cattle compete for the same forage and lower it, and adding bison changes the population, not the capacity of the habitat.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ035
**Answer: D.** The two points at 90% and 96% enrollment have TFRs of 1.8 and 1.5, both below 2.1. The data show an association across ten countries, so they cannot guarantee a specific effect in any one country. The country with a TFR of 4.6 has only 41% enrollment, and the overall trend is clearly downward, not flat.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ036
**Answer: B.** Past high fertility created a large cohort of young people who are now having children. Even though each woman now averages only enough children to replace herself and her partner, the number of births stays high because there are so many women of childbearing age, so growth continues for decades (population momentum). A TFR of 2.1 is the replacement level, and TFR is a measure of births per woman, not a measure that omits any group.
:::

:::sol QB-U3-MCQ037
**Answer: B.** Carrying capacity = 150,000 kg ÷ 1,500 kg per elk = 100 elk. Excess = 130 − 100 = 30 elk, and 30 ÷ 100 × 100 = 30% above K. Dividing by the current herd (30 ÷ 130) gives 23%, the herd being 130% of K is not the percent by which it exceeds K, and 77% is K as a percent of the herd.

:::sol QB-U3-FRQ001

(b) If supplemental forage is provided to some winter ranges, then the goat populations in those ranges will be larger (or will have higher survival and more kids per female) than in ranges without supplemental forage, because winter forage limits the population. *(1 point: a prediction that links the independent and dependent variables and is testable)*

(c) *(1 point each)*
  (i) Independent variable: whether or not a winter range receives supplemental forage pellets (or the amount of pellets).
  (ii) Dependent variable, any one: number of goats per range; kid-to-adult ratio; winter survival of adults; body mass or condition of goats.
  (iii) Controlled variable, any one: size and elevation of the winter ranges; amount of natural forage per hectare at the start; absence of hunting; method and timing of the goat counts; predator density; human disturbance.

(d) Any two, *(1 point each)*: replicate (three ranges per treatment, as proposed, or more) so that chance differences average out; assign treatments to ranges that are similar in size, elevation, and starting forage (or assign at random) to avoid bias; run for several winters so that one unusual winter does not decide the outcome; use the same counting method, observers, and time of year in all ranges; include the no-supplement ranges as a control group.

:::sol QB-U3-FRQ003
(a) Net change = births + immigrants − deaths − emigrants = 125,000 + 20,000 − 45,000 − 5,000 = 95,000 people per year. Growth rate = 95,000 ÷ 5,000,000 × 100 = **1.9% per year**. *(1 point for the net change including all four terms; 1 point for the correct percentage)*

(b) RNI = (births − deaths) ÷ population × 100 = (125,000 − 45,000) ÷ 5,000,000 × 100 = 80,000 ÷ 5,000,000 × 100 = **1.6% per year**. (Equivalent: CBR 25 − CDR 9 = 16 per 1,000.) *(1 point; do not credit if migration is included)*

(c) Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 1.9 = **37 years** (36.8). Population after one doubling time ≈ 2 × 5.0 million = **10 million** people. *(1 point for the doubling time; 1 point for the population)*

(d) Demand = 5,000,000 people × 0.55 kg/person/day × 365 days/year ÷ 1,000 kg/metric ton ≈ 1,003,750 t ≈ **1.0 × 10⁶ metric tons per year**. *(1 point; units must be shown. Accept 1,003,750 or 1.0 million.)*

(e) Shortfall = demand − production ≈ 1,003,750 − 850,000 ≈ 153,750 t, or about **1.5 × 10⁵ metric tons per year** (about 15% of demand). *(1 point; accept 150,000 to 154,000 t, or a value consistent with part (d))*

(f) Any one *(1 point)*: as incomes rise and girls' education and access to family planning expand, women marry and have children later and have fewer children, lowering birth rates; falling infant mortality lowers the number of births parents need; urbanization raises the cost of raising children; or the country moves from Stage 2 to Stage 3 of the demographic transition. 

(g) *(1 point for a reasonable solution; 1 point for a justification that uses the quantitative results)* Examples: raise yields per hectare with improved seed, irrigation, and efficient fertilizer use and reduce post-harvest losses; slow population growth through education for girls and family planning; import grain or diversify the diet. Justification: the shortfall is already about 1.5 × 10⁵ t per year, and if per-capita consumption stays the same, demand will double to about 2.0 × 10⁶ t in about 37 years, which is about 2.4 times current production, so a one-time fix would not be enough; reducing growth lowers future demand while yield improvements raise supply.
:::

## Unit 7 — Atmospheric Pollution

### Section A — MCQ Answers and Explanations

:::sol QB-U7-MCQ001
**Answer: C.** A secondary pollutant is not emitted directly; it forms in the atmosphere from reactions among other pollutants. Ground-level ozone forms when nitrogen oxides and VOCs react in sunlight. Carbon monoxide, lead, and sulfur dioxide (A, B, D) are all released directly from their sources, so they are primary pollutants.
:::

:::sol QB-U7-MCQ006
**Answer: D.** Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides react with water in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids. CO₂ and CH₄ (A) are greenhouse gases, CO and O₃ (B) are not the main acid precursors, and lead and mercury (C) are toxic metals rather than acid-forming gases.

:::sol QB-U7-MCQ007

:::sol QB-U7-MCQ008

:::sol QB-U7-FRQ001
(a) Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as formaldehyde, are the most likely cause. Sources: carpet and its adhesive, particleboard cabinets, or paint. *(1 point: a correct pollutant category with a matching building material)*

(b) Example: "If the renovation materials release VOCs, then the formaldehyde concentration in Wing B classrooms will be higher than in Wing A classrooms." *(1 point: testable, if/then, states the direction)*

(c) Independent variable: the wing (renovated Wing B versus non-renovated Wing A). Dependent variable: concentration of formaldehyde or total VOCs in the air. Sample at the same times on several days (for example, mornings and afternoons for five school days) with the ventilation set the same way. Repeat the measurement to account for variability, and compare the wing averages. Alternative: include an outdoor sample as a baseline. *(1 point: monitors in all classrooms in both wings at the same height/conditions; 1 point: sampled over multiple days or times; 1 point: replication or averaging, or a baseline sample, that addresses variability)*

(e) The mean VOC or formaldehyde concentration in Wing B would be clearly higher than in Wing A, beyond the variation within each wing. *(1 point)*

:::sol QB-U7-FRQ002
(a) As SO₂ emissions decreased from 480 to 132 thousand metric tons per year, the pH of Lake Alder increased from 4.6 to 5.7, so lower emissions are associated with less acidic water (an inverse relationship). *(1 point: describes the inverse relationship with direction or data)*

(b) Percent decrease = (480 − 132) ÷ 480 × 100 = 348 ÷ 480 × 100 = 72.5%. *(1 point: the correct value, 72.5% or about 73%)*

(c) Limestone bedrock contains carbonate minerals (calcium carbonate) that react with and neutralize acid deposition. This buffering capacity keeps the lake water near neutral. Lake Alder is on granite and thin soils, which have little buffering capacity. *(1 point: limestone or carbonate neutralizes acid; 1 point: ties this to a stable pH despite the high emissions, or contrasts with granite)*

(d) SO₂ from the stack reacts with oxygen and water in the atmosphere to form sulfuric acid. Wind carries it downwind and it falls as acid rain (wet deposition) or as dry particles and gases (dry deposition). *(1 point: SO₂ reacts to form an acid (sulfuric acid) and is carried downwind and deposited)*

(g) Examples: switch the plant to natural gas or renewables; reduce NOₓ from vehicles by adding converters or promoting electric cars (NOₓ also forms nitric acid); add lime to the lake (liming); reduce energy use through conservation. :::sol QB-U7-FRQ004
(a) Inversion days (Dec 4, 5, 6, 9) had much higher PM2.5 (61–92 µg/m³) than non-inversion days (12–23 µg/m³). The presence of an inversion is associated with higher PM2.5. *(1 point: describes the positive association using data)*

(b) Inversion days: (61 + 78 + 92 + 85) ÷ 4 = 316 ÷ 4 = 79 µg/m³. Non-inversion days: (14 + 18 + 23 + 12) ÷ 4 = 67 ÷ 4 = 16.75 ≈ 16.8 µg/m³. *(1 point: inversion mean; 1 point: non-inversion mean)*

(c) During an inversion a layer of warmer air lies above the cooler surface air. The warm layer acts as a lid, so the cool, dense surface air cannot rise and mix with the air above. Pollutants from vehicles, heating, and industry stay trapped in a shallow layer near the ground, and their concentrations build up over time. *(1 point: warm air layer above cooler air prevents vertical mixing; 1 point: pollutants trapped and accumulate near the ground)*

(d) Any one: wood-burning stoves and fireplaces; vehicle exhaust, including diesel; industrial combustion; construction dust; burning of coal or oil for heat. *(1 point)*

(e) Any one: respiratory irritation, asthma attacks, reduced lung function, bronchitis, heart disease, or premature death. *(1 point)*

(f) Examples: a temporary ban on wood burning on forecast inversion days; encourage public transit or free bus rides; restrict industrial emissions or require diesel idling limits on those days. Justification: these actions reduce the amount of PM2.5 emitted into the trapped air, so concentrations build up less. *(1 point: valid action tied to forecast inversion days; 1 point: justification linking it to lower emissions or concentrations)*

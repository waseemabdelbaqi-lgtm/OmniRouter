## Unit 1 — The Living World: Ecosystems

:::sol SG-U1-E001
**Answer: (a) resource partitioning; (b) it separates the species' use of the seeds so they rarely compete; (c) competition increases.**

(a) The four species feed at different plant heights (0–10, 10–30, 30–60 and 60–100 cm), so they share the seed supply by **resource partitioning** (here, spatial partitioning).

(b) Each species takes seeds from a different layer, so a seed eaten by one species is not one that another species needed. Overlap in resource use is small, so interspecific competition is weak and each species keeps enough food to survive and reproduce.

(c) **Competition increases.** Half as many seeds means each bird must find food from a smaller supply, so individuals within each species (intraspecific) and, where feeding zones touch, between species compete harder for what is left. A common wrong answer says competition falls because "there is less food"; scarcity makes interactions stronger, not weaker.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E002
**Answer: (a) A = tropical rainforest, B = tundra, C = desert; (b) decomposition is fast in A and very slow in B.**

(a) Station A (25 °C, 300 cm/yr) is hot and very wet: **tropical rainforest**. Station B (−9 °C, 22 cm/yr) is very cold with low precipitation: **tundra**. Station C (18 °C, 12 cm/yr) has precipitation under about 30 cm/yr: **desert**, even though it is not especially hot.

(b) At Station A, warmth and moisture make decomposers very active, so dead matter is broken down quickly and nutrients are returned and taken up by plants almost at once. Little is stored in the soil, which is thin and nutrient-poor, and turnover is fast. At Station B, the cold (and permafrost) slows decomposers, so dead matter decays slowly, organic matter accumulates and nutrients are released and recycled slowly.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E003
**Answer: (a) Site X = coral reef; Site Y = estuary; (b) clear water lets light reach the producers; Site Y offers abundant nutrients and shelter.**

(a) Site X (salinity 34 ppt, warm, 8 m deep, clear, corals) is a **coral reef**. Site Y (12 ppt, where a river meets a bay, sediment-laden, nutrient-rich, young fish and oysters) is an **estuary**, with salinity between fresh and marine.

(b) Low turbidity at Site X lets sunlight penetrate to the algae living in the corals and to other producers, so photosynthesis can occur throughout the shallow water and primary productivity stays high. Site Y supports many young fish because it is shallow and sheltered, and its high nutrient supply sustains abundant producers and food; the cloudy water also helps hide the young from predators. Either reason (nutrients/food or shelter) earns the point.

:::sol SG-U1-E004
**Answer: (a) combustion → photosynthesis → feeding → cellular respiration; (b) source; the coal carbon had been buried for millions of years.**

(a) In order:
1. **Combustion** of the coal: carbon in coal (fossil organic carbon) becomes CO₂ in the atmosphere.
2. **Photosynthesis** in the wheat: CO₂ becomes organic carbon (glucose and other compounds) in the wheat plant.
3. **Feeding** (the cow eats the wheat): carbon is now organic compounds in the cow's body.
4. **Cellular respiration** in the cow: organic carbon is oxidized and leaves as CO₂ in the exhaled breath.

(b) The coal seam acted as a **source**, because it released carbon to the atmosphere. Before the plant was built that carbon was locked in a long-term reservoir (buried, compressed remains of ancient organisms) for millions of years, so it was not cycling among the atmosphere, organisms and surface ocean. Burning it added "new" carbon to the fast cycle.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E005
**Answer: (a) denitrification, with nitrogen gas (N₂) leaving; (b) nitrate is the main nitrogen form roots absorb; (c) ammonification.**

(a) Waterlogged soil is low in oxygen, which favors **denitrification**: bacteria convert nitrate (NO₃⁻) to **nitrogen gas (N₂)**, which escapes to the atmosphere. (Leaching of dissolved nitrate also contributes to loss, but the process asked for is denitrification.)

(b) Nitrate is the main form of nitrogen plant roots absorb, and nitrogen is needed for proteins, DNA and chlorophyll. With less nitrate, the corn makes less chlorophyll (yellowing) and less protein (stunted growth), so nitrogen limits production.

(c) **Ammonification** (decay): decomposers convert the nitrogen in the dead leaves to ammonium (NH₄⁺), which plants can take up or which nitrifying bacteria can convert to nitrate.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E006
**Answer: (a) phosphorus; (b) 280% increase; (c) phosphorus has no atmospheric source and is released only by slow rock weathering.**

(a) **Phosphorus** is the limiting nutrient. Adding phosphorus alone raised algae from 10 to 38 µg/L, a large response, while nitrogen alone barely changed it (11 µg/L). Adding both gave only a little more (40 µg/L) than phosphorus alone, so nitrogen is not the main limit; once phosphorus is supplied, growth rises sharply.

(b) Percent increase = (new − original) ÷ original × 100 = (38 − 10) µg/L ÷ 10 µg/L × 100 = 28 ÷ 10 × 100 = **280%**.

(c) Phosphorus has no significant atmospheric component, so it cannot be added from the air as nitrogen can be fixed from N₂. It enters ecosystems mainly by the very slow weathering of phosphorus-bearing rock, and much of it settles out into sediments, so little is available in the water.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E007
**Answer: (a) 8.0 × 10⁶ m³/yr; (b) evapotranspiration, with plant transpiration as a biological contributor.**

(a) Precipitation depth = 80 cm = 0.80 m. Area = 25 km² × 10⁶ m²/km² = 2.5 × 10⁷ m².
Precipitation volume = 0.80 m × 2.5 × 10⁷ m² = 2.0 × 10⁷ m³/yr.
Stream flow = 40% (100% − 60%) of that: 0.40 × 2.0 × 10⁷ m³/yr = **8.0 × 10⁶ m³/yr**.

(b) **Evapotranspiration** carries away the largest portion (60%). Biological contributor: **transpiration**, the release of water vapor from plant leaves (evaporation from soil and surfaces is the non-biological part).
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E008
**Answer: (a) NPP of A = 1,800 and B = 1,800 kcal/m²/yr; (b) 30% and 75%; (c) the claim is not supported.**

(a) NPP = GPP − R.
Ecosystem A: 6,000 − 4,200 = **1,800 kcal/m²/yr**.
Ecosystem B: 2,400 − 600 = **1,800 kcal/m²/yr**.

(b) NPP ÷ GPP × 100.
A: 1,800 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **30%**.
B: 1,800 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **75%**.

(c) The claim is **not supported**. The energy available to consumers is NPP, not GPP, and both ecosystems have the same NPP (1,800 kcal/m²/yr). Ecosystem A's higher GPP is mostly spent on the producers' own respiration (70% of GPP), so it supports no more consumers than Ecosystem B, other things being equal.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E009
**Answer: (a) 25 kcal/m²/yr; (b) 40,000 kcal/m²/yr; (c) each transfer loses energy as heat.**

(a) Three transfers (phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → tuna), each × 0.10:
phytoplankton 25,000 → zooplankton 2,500 → small fish 250 → tuna 25 kcal/m²/yr. Check: 25,000 × (0.10)³ = **25 kcal/m²/yr**.

(b) Go down two steps from small fish to phytoplankton, dividing by 0.10 each time:
400 ÷ 0.10 = 4,000 kcal/m²/yr of zooplankton; 4,000 ÷ 0.10 = **40,000 kcal/m²/yr** of phytoplankton.

(c) By the second law of thermodynamics, every energy conversion is less than 100% efficient and some energy becomes low-quality heat (mostly through the respiration the small fish use to move, grow and stay alive). Only the energy stored in the small fish's tissues, about 10%, can be eaten by the tuna, so the tuna receive less than the small fish received.

:::sol SG-U1-E010
**Answer: (a) grass → grasshoppers → lizards → hawks; the hawk is a tertiary consumer; (b) grasshoppers increase; (c) negative feedback.**

(a) The longest chain is **grass → grasshoppers → lizards → hawks** (four levels). Grass is the producer (level 1), grasshoppers are primary consumers (level 2), lizards are secondary consumers (level 3) and the hawk is a **tertiary consumer** (level 4) in this chain. (In the shorter chain grass → rabbits → hawks the hawk is a secondary consumer, so it occupies more than one level in the web.)

(b) The grasshopper population **increases**. Lizards are their only predator, so removing the lizards removes the main source of mortality; grasshoppers survive and reproduce more, limited only by food or other factors. (Hawks would then rely more on rabbits, which can affect the rabbits as well.)

(c) This is **negative (balancing) feedback**. A rise in rabbits increases the foxes' food and fox numbers, and more foxes then eat more rabbits, which reverses the original rise and pushes rabbits back down, stabilizing the system.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E011
**Answer: (a) M = savanna, N = temperate grassland; (b) the dry season selects for drought-tolerant, fire-adapted plants; (c) N would shift toward shrubland or desert.**

(a) **Station M is a savanna.** Evidence: (1) temperature is warm all year (24–28 °C, almost no seasonal change); (2) rainfall is strongly seasonal, with a wet season (150–230 mm/month, November–April) and a long dry season (under 15 mm/month, May–October). A rainforest would have rain in every month.
**Station N is a temperate grassland.** Evidence: (1) a cold winter (−6 °C in January) and a warm summer (20 °C in July), a large seasonal temperature range; (2) low-to-moderate precipitation (15–25 mm in winter), with rainfall peaking in summer (60–90 mm, May–August). Too little rain for forest, too much for desert.

(b) Plants in the savanna must survive six months with almost no rain. Grasses die back or go dormant in the dry season and regrow when rains return, trees are scattered and often drop their leaves or have deep roots, and dry grass fuels fires, so the plants that dominate are drought-tolerant and fire-adapted; the dry season also limits tree density so grasses dominate.

(c) If the region warmed and became drier, evaporation would rise and soil moisture would fall, so tall grasses would be replaced by more drought-tolerant shrubs and short grasses. The biome would move toward **shrubland or, if precipitation fell low enough (under about 30 cm/yr), desert**, because biomes are determined by temperature and precipitation. Station N's biome boundary would shift accordingly.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E012
**Answer: (a) 13,200 Mg CO₂; (b) draining lets oxygen in and speeds aerobic decomposition; (c) a source.**

(a) Carbon stored = 50 ha × 600 Mg C/ha = 30,000 Mg C.
Carbon oxidized = 0.12 × 30,000 Mg C = 3,600 Mg C.
Mass of CO₂ = mass of C × 44 ÷ 12 = 3,600 Mg × 3.67 = **13,200 Mg CO₂** (about 1.3 × 10⁴ Mg; exactly 3,600 × 44 ÷ 12 = 13,200).

(b) Waterlogged peat has little oxygen, so decomposition is very slow and organic matter accumulates. Draining (and plowing) lets air into the soil, so aerobic decomposers become active and oxidize the stored organic carbon to CO₂.

(c) The peatland is now a **source** of carbon: it releases CO₂ to the atmosphere instead of accumulating carbon.
:::

:::sol SG-U1-E013
**Answer: (a) 250 years; (b) −3 km³/yr, shrinking; (c) pavement reduces infiltration and increases runoff.**

(a) Residence time = volume stored ÷ rate of inflow = 3.0 × 10³ km³ ÷ 12 km³/yr = **250 years**.

(b) Change in storage = inflow − outflow = 9 km³/yr − 12 km³/yr = **−3 km³/yr**. The aquifer is **shrinking** by 3 km³ each year.

(c) Pavement is impermeable, so rain cannot soak into the soil (infiltration and percolation fall). More of the precipitation leaves quickly as surface runoff, less water reaches the aquifer, and recharge drops.

## Unit 9 — Global Change

:::sol SG-U9-E001
**Answer: (a) the fall in stratospheric ozone. (b) Skin cancer or cataracts from UV; lung irritation from ground-level ozone.**

(a) Stratospheric ozone absorbs most incoming UV-B and UV-C and converts the energy to heat. When its concentration falls, less UV is absorbed and more reaches the surface and the residents. Ground-level ozone sits in the troposphere, below the protective layer, so a rise in it does not change the UV arriving at the city. A common wrong answer says the rise in ground-level ozone "adds" UV; it does not, and it is a pollutant, not a shield.

(b) Increased UV: higher risk of skin cancer (UV damages DNA), cataracts, or immune suppression (any one). Increased ground-level ozone: irritated airways and lungs, triggering asthma, coughing and reduced lung function (any one).
:::

:::sol SG-U9-E002
**Answer: (a) Coolant X. (b) Coolant Y. (c) Coolant Z is the best choice, with leak and ignition controls.**

(a) Coolant X is the only one containing chlorine. If it leaks, it can rise into the stratosphere, where UV frees chlorine atoms (Cl). Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂, then ClO + O → Cl + O₂ regenerates the Cl, so each chlorine atom acts as a catalyst and destroys thousands of ozone molecules. Y and Z have no chlorine, so they cannot run this cycle.

(b) Coolant Y has the highest heat-trapping ability (GWP) per molecule, so per kilogram released it adds most to the greenhouse effect. X and Z have low GWP.

(c) Claim: choose Z. Reasoning: it has zero ozone harm (no chlorine) and low climate impact, whereas X harms ozone and Y is a potent greenhouse gas. Its flammability is a safety risk that can be managed. Precaution (any one): leak detectors and alarms, ventilation, keeping ignition sources away from the plant, sealed low-charge systems, staff training. Choosing Y with a leak-recovery plan could be argued but is weaker because climate harm is built into every leak.
:::

:::sol SG-U9-E006
**Answer: (a) positive (ice-albedo) feedback. (b) Bright clouds counteract the warming, so it is negative. (c) Loss of hunting or resting platform for an ice-dependent species.**

(a) Warming lengthens the ice-free period, exposing dark water. Dark water has a low albedo, so it absorbs more sunlight than the bright ice it replaced. The extra absorbed energy warms the water and air, which lengthens the ice-free period further. The change (warming and ice loss) is amplified, so it is a positive feedback loop.

(b) Warmer air increases evaporation, forming bright low clouds that reflect more sunlight to space, which reduces the sunlight reaching the surface and so reduces the warming. The result opposes the original change, so it is a negative feedback loop. (Positive and negative describe direction, not whether the outcome is good or bad.)

(c) Any one: polar bears and seals lose sea-ice platforms for hunting, resting and breeding; under-ice algae at the base of the food web are lost; ice-dependent fish or waterfowl lose habitat as the ice season shortens.
:::

:::sol SG-U9-E014
**Answer: (a) 80 units. (b) 62 units. (c) Energy out falls to 96 against 100 in, so Earth warms until a warmer surface emits more infrared (negative feedback) and out again equals 100.**

(a) Surface budget: energy in = energy out. Surface absorbs 50 from the Sun + infrared from the atmosphere = 130 emitted. Returned infrared = 130 − 50 = **80 units**.

(b) The atmosphere absorbs the 22 units of sunlight plus the infrared that does not escape directly: 130 − 10 = 120. Total absorbed = 22 + 120 = 142 units. It emits 142 in total: 80 down, so to space = 142 − 80 = **62 units**.
Check at the top of the atmosphere: reflected 28 + direct infrared 10 + atmosphere's 62 = 100, equal to the 100 arriving.

(c) Immediate effect: only 6 units now escape directly, so the atmosphere absorbs 4 more. Before the atmosphere and surface adjust, outgoing energy = 28 + 6 + 62 = 96 units, less than the 100 arriving. About 4 units per 100 are retained, so Earth is out of balance and gains energy. Long term: the surface and lower atmosphere warm. Feedback: a warmer surface emits more infrared (and the atmosphere more to space); this negative feedback continues until outgoing energy again equals 100 at a higher temperature. Other positive feedbacks (water vapor, ice-albedo) would amplify the warming along the way.
:::

:::sol SG-U9-E016
**Answer (model design):** remove the plant from replicated, randomly chosen bays and compare DO and native fish species with untouched bays.

(a) Hypothesis: If the floating invasive plant is removed from a bay, then dissolved oxygen and the number of native fish species will be higher than in bays where the plant is left in place.

(b) Independent variable: presence or absence of the invasive plant (removal versus no removal). Dependent variables: dissolved oxygen (mg/L) and native fish species richness (number of species).

(c) Design: choose, for example, 12 similar bays and randomly assign 6 to plant removal (treatment) and 6 to be left alone (control group). Replication (6 per group) lets chance differences average out. Measure DO with the same meter at the same depth and time of day, and sample fish by the same method (nets or traps, same effort) before removal and at set intervals (for instance monthly) for a full season or longer. Concurrent controls allow the treatment effect to be separated from year-to-year changes. Alternative acceptable designs: matched pairs of bays (one removed, one control), or a survey along a gradient of plant cover as an observational study (which shows association only).

(d) Variables kept similar (any two): bay size and depth, water temperature, water clarity/light, nutrient levels, season and sampling time, shoreline development, connection to the main lake, and sampling method.

(e) Supporting pattern: after removal, treatment bays show higher DO and more native fish species than control bays, with a gap that grows over time and is bigger than the variation among replicates; control bays show no change. Limitation (any one): small number of bays, one lake, short time, fish moving between bays, or unmeasured differences such as pollution or fish stocking; a survey without removal cannot show cause.
:::

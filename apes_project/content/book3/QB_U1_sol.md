## Unit 1 — The Living World: Ecosystems

### Section A — MCQ Answers and Explanations

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ001
**Answer: B.** Both partners gain (the tree gets phosphorus and water, the fungus gets sugars), which defines mutualism. Commensalism (A) requires one species to be unaffected, and parasitism (C) requires harm to one partner; neither fits a relationship in which both grow better.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ002
**Answer: D.** Permafrost, a very short growing season and mosses, lichens and low shrubs describe tundra. Taiga (A) is also cold, but it is a coniferous forest with trees and no continuous permafrost layer controlling vegetation.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ003
**Answer: A.** The oceans are the primary reservoir of water at Earth's surface. Ice caps (B) and groundwater (C) are much smaller reservoirs, even though ice holds most of the fresh water.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ004
**Answer: C.** Photosynthesis takes CO₂ from the air and builds it into sugars. Cellular respiration (A) and combustion (D) release carbon to the atmosphere, and decomposition (B) returns carbon from dead matter.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ005
**Answer: A.** In nitrogen fixation, certain bacteria convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonia (which becomes ammonium in soil). B describes denitrification, C describes assimilation, and D describes ammonification.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ006
**Answer: C.** The phosphorus cycle lacks a significant atmospheric component; its major reservoirs are rocks and ocean sediments. A is wrong because the nitrogen reservoir is atmospheric but phosphorus has none; D is wrong because phosphorus is scarce, since rocks weather slowly.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ007
**Answer: D.** Counting up from the producer: grass is level 1, grasshopper (primary consumer) level 2, frog (secondary) level 3, snake (tertiary) level 4. Option B confuses the snake with the frog.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ008
**Answer: B.** An estuary is where river freshwater meets the sea, so its salinity varies with tides and flow. A lake (D) stays fresh, and coral reefs and the open ocean (A, C) have stable, high salinity.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ009
**Answer: C.** Producers → primary consumers → secondary consumers is two transfers: 50,000 × 0.10 × 0.10 = 500 kcal/m²/yr. D (5,000) counts only one transfer and gives the energy for primary consumers.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ010
**Answer: A.** NPP = GPP − respiration = 6,000 − 4,500 = 1,500 kcal/m²/yr. D adds the two values instead of subtracting, and C ignores respiration.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ011
**Answer: D.** Species that use the same resource (insects) in different places on the tree are partitioning resources, which reduces competition and allows coexistence. B is wrong because the competition described is between species (interspecific), not within one species.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ012
**Answer: B.** Temperatures are warm all year (24–29 °C) with a pronounced wet season (about November to April) and a dry season (June to August), typical of a savanna. A tropical rainforest (A) would be wet in every month, and a temperate grassland (C) would have a cold winter.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ013
**Answer: C.** Marine resources such as fish vary with nutrient availability, and nitrate is highest at Site X (18 µmol/L), so phytoplankton at the base of the food web should be most abundant there. Site W has little nitrate (A, D), and the very turbid water at Site Y limits light for photosynthesis, so B is wrong. Warm water does not hold more nutrients.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ014
**Answer: A.** Inputs to the atmosphere: B + D + E = 118 + 90 + 10 = 218 Gt C/yr. Outputs: A + C = 120 + 92 = 212 Gt C/yr. Net change = 218 − 212 = +6 Gt C/yr. Option C (+10) counts only the fossil fuel flux, ignoring that the land and ocean fluxes nearly balance, and B (+8) is not the difference between total inputs and outputs.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ015
**Answer: D.** Step T moves nitrate to N₂ gas in the atmosphere, which is denitrification; it is carried out by bacteria in oxygen-poor, waterlogged soil. Step Q (nitrification) needs oxygen and would slow down, and P (fixation) and S (ammonification) add nitrogen to the soil rather than remove it.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ016
**Answer: B.** Adding phosphorus alone raised chlorophyll *a* from 4.1 to 17.8 µg/L, while nitrogen alone changed almost nothing (4.5), so phosphorus is the main limiting nutrient; once phosphorus was supplied, nitrogen became limiting and the combination gave the highest value (41.3). A fails because the 0.4 µg/L increase is negligible, and D fails because phosphorus alone caused a large increase.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ017
**Answer: A.** Pavement and roofs prevent infiltration, so more precipitation becomes surface runoff and drains quickly to streams. B and D state the opposite, and C is wrong because replacing plants removes transpiration.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ018
**Answer: C.** Total NPP = NPP per m² × area. Open ocean: 600 × 3.6 × 10¹⁴ m² = 2.2 × 10¹⁷ kcal/yr; rainforest: 9,000 × 1.7 × 10¹³ = 1.5 × 10¹⁷; estuary: 8,000 × 1.4 × 10¹² = 1.1 × 10¹⁶. The ocean has the lowest NPP per area yet the largest total. A is the tempting error of ignoring area.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ019
**Answer: B.** Matter is conserved and cycles between organisms and the environment through decomposers, whereas energy enters as sunlight, flows one way through trophic levels, and is lost as heat. A and C wrongly make energy cycle, and D wrongly makes matter leave the ecosystem.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ020
**Answer: D.** Pike are tertiary consumers, three transfers from the algae: 2.5 × 10⁶ ÷ 0.10 ÷ 0.10 ÷ 0.10 = 2.5 × 10⁹ kcal/yr. Option C counts only two transfers.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ021
**Answer: C.** The snake eats voles (primary consumers, so the snake is a secondary consumer at level 3) and frogs (secondary consumers, so the snake is a tertiary consumer at level 4). The grasshopper and clover each feed at one level only, and the frog eats only grasshoppers.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ022
**Answer: A.** In this web the frogs are the only predator of grasshoppers, so removing the frogs releases grasshoppers from predation and their numbers rise. B and D invent relationships that are not in the web (frogs are predators of grasshoppers and prey of snakes, not competitors of grasshoppers), and C contradicts the web, which shows grasshoppers eating only grass.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ023
**Answer: D.** Biome distribution is dynamic and follows climate; warming lets the taiga move poleward into tundra. A, B and C all require movement opposite to the warming.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ024
**Answer: B.** Algae in marine biomes supply a large share of Earth's oxygen and take CO₂ from the atmosphere. A, C and D describe processes that algae do not carry out; photosynthesis releases oxygen, it does not absorb it.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ025
**Answer: C.** Residence time = reservoir volume ÷ flow rate = 8.0 × 10³ km³ ÷ 16 km³/yr = 500 years. A inverts the ratio, and D multiplies the two numbers.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ026
**Answer: A.** Blue-green light penetrates to depth while red light is absorbed near the surface, so deep-living photosynthesizers have pigments that capture the remaining wavelengths. Photosynthesis requires light (D), and red light is gone at 40 m (B).
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ027
**Answer: B.** Transfer efficiencies are 1,800 ÷ 20,000 = 9.0%, 160 ÷ 1,800 = 8.9% and 12 ÷ 160 = 7.5%, all near 10%. The remainder is lost as heat in metabolism, as the second law predicts. C is wrong because energy is conserved: it changes form rather than being destroyed.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ028
**Answer: D.** Energy in harvested bass: 60 kg × 2,000 kcal/kg = 1.2 × 10⁵ kcal/yr. Bass are three transfers above the algae: 1.2 × 10⁵ ÷ 0.10³ = 1.2 × 10⁸ kcal/yr of NPP. Area = 1.2 × 10⁸ ÷ 2,400 kcal/m²/yr = 5.0 × 10⁴ m² = 5.0 ha. Option C results from counting only two transfers.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ029
**Answer: C.** During the Northern Hemisphere growing season, photosynthesis by land plants exceeds respiration, lowering CO₂; the steady rise (about 2.4 ppm per year) reflects fossil fuel burning. A is wrong because summer fossil fuel use does not drop, and D reverses the logic since net uptake exceeds decomposition in summer.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ030
**Answer: A.** A valid test has replicated treatment and control plots that differ only in nitrogen. B has no replication and confounds nitrogen with location, C changes three factors at once, and D has no control group to separate nitrogen from year-to-year weather.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ031
**Answer: B.** Bass fell, minnows rose, zooplankton fell and chlorophyll *a* rose: a trophic cascade in which fewer predators allow more minnows, which eat more zooplankton, which graze less on algae. A reverses the order shown by the data, and D fails because the stem gives no evidence that bass eat zooplankton; the zooplankton fell as minnows (their predators) rose.
:::

:::sol QB-U1-MCQ032
**Answer: D.** Precipitation is the same, evapotranspiration fell by 250 mm (700 → 450), and runoff rose by 600 − 380 = 220 mm; with less water returned to the atmosphere, more leaves as streamflow (the rest, +30 mm, goes to storage). A is wrong because precipitation is equal; B is the tempting error, since clearing reduces infiltration, not increases it.

### Section B — FRQ Solutions

:::sol QB-U1-FRQ001
(a) Eutrophication or excess nutrient pollution of the lake, producing summer algal blooms (algal scum, odor). *(1 point: identifies nutrient-driven algal growth as the problem.)*

(b) For example: "If phosphorus is the limiting nutrient, then bottles with added phosphorus will have more algal growth than bottles with only nitrogen added." Reasoning: phosphorus is scarce because rocks weather slowly and it has no atmospheric source, and the July water has a very high nitrogen to phosphorus ratio (0.90 mg/L N compared with 0.008 mg/L P). A nitrogen-limitation hypothesis with reasoning about fertilizer input and the nitrogen cycle is also acceptable if it is testable and justified. *(1 point: testable if-then or directional prediction with reasoning.)*

(c) (i) The nutrient treatment (nitrogen added, phosphorus added, both added, none). *(1 point)*
(ii) Algal growth, measured as chlorophyll *a* concentration (or algal cell count or biomass). *(1 point)*

(d) Collect lake water and fill many identical clear bottles. Make four treatments: no nutrients (control), nitrogen only, phosphorus only, nitrogen and phosphorus. Use several replicate bottles (at least three) per treatment, and incubate them together in the lake or under the same lights. Measure algal growth at the start and after one to three weeks. *(1 point: treatments with a control; 1 point: replication/same conditions for reliable results.)*

(e) Light (or temperature, bottle size, volume of water, incubation time, water source). Different light or temperature would change algal growth independently of the nutrient, so the effect of nutrients could not be isolated. *(1 point: names variable with a reason linked to isolating the nutrient effect.)*

(f) The phosphorus-only bottles (and the nitrogen-and-phosphorus bottles) would show significantly higher algal growth than the control and nitrogen-only bottles. (If the hypothesis was nitrogen limitation, the corresponding result for nitrogen.) *(1 point)*

(g) Any one with justification, for example: establish vegetated buffer strips along the inlet stream so that phosphorus-rich soil and manure are trapped before reaching the lake; or reduce fertilizer and manure application and match it to crop needs. Justification: because phosphorus has no atmospheric source, the lake's supply depends on runoff and sediment, so cutting phosphorus inputs lowers the nutrient that limits algae. *(1 point: reasonable action; 1 point: justification tied to the phosphorus (or nitrogen) cycle.)*
:::

:::sol QB-U1-FRQ002
(a) GPP is lowest in winter, rises rapidly from March to a peak in June–July (about 1,500–1,550 kcal/m²/month), then falls rapidly through autumn to a low in December. *(1 point: describes rise to a summer peak and decline, using data.)*

(b) Any two: longer days and higher solar radiation in July provide more light energy for photosynthesis; warmer temperatures in July speed enzyme activity and photosynthesis; the deciduous trees have full leaf area in July but no leaves in January; more liquid water is available in summer than when it is frozen in winter. *(1 point each, maximum 2; each must link the factor to photosynthesis.)*

(c) November (120 < 130), December (40 < 90), January (40 < 90) and February (80 < 90). NPP = GPP − respiration, so it is negative when plants respire more (using stored energy to maintain living tissue, such as trunks, roots and evergreen needles) than they capture in photosynthesis. *(1 point: identifies months; 1 point: explains that respiration exceeds GPP.)*

(d) Total GPP = 40 + 80 + 260 + 600 + 1,100 + 1,500 + 1,550 + 1,400 + 900 + 400 + 120 + 40 = 7,990 kcal/m²/yr. Total respiration = 90 + 90 + 150 + 280 + 450 + 620 + 700 + 650 + 480 + 260 + 130 + 90 = 3,990 kcal/m²/yr. Percentage = (3,990 ÷ 7,990) × 100 = 49.9% (about 50%). *(1 point: both totals; 1 point: correct percentage.)*

(e) The plants gain energy: annual NPP = 7,990 − 3,990 = 4,000 kcal/m²/yr, which is positive. *(1 point: claim with supporting number.)*

(f) GPP would decrease substantially in those months because less leaf area means less photosynthesis; annual NPP would decrease because plant respiration (maintenance of living tissue) does not fall in proportion to the leaf loss. *(1 point: predicts decrease with justification.)*

(g) For example: manage insect pests, protect forest from clearing, or thin and replant with diverse species so that leaves capture light every growing season; justified by the link between leaf area and GPP. *(1 point: reasonable action with justification.)*
:::

:::sol QB-U1-FRQ003
(a) Area = 400 ha × 10⁴ m²/ha = 4.0 × 10⁶ m². Total NPP = 4.0 × 10⁶ m² × 4,500 kcal/m²/yr = 1.8 × 10¹⁰ kcal/yr. *(1 point)*

(b) Cattle (primary consumers): 1.8 × 10¹⁰ × 0.10 = 1.8 × 10⁹ kcal/yr. People (secondary consumers): 1.8 × 10⁹ × 0.10 = 1.8 × 10⁸ kcal/yr. *(1 point: cattle energy; 1 point: energy available to people.)*

(c) Annual need per person = 2,500 kcal/day × 365 days = 912,500 kcal/yr (9.1 × 10⁵). People = 1.8 × 10⁸ ÷ 9.125 × 10⁵ = 197 people, about 2.0 × 10² people. *(1 point: annual need per person; 1 point: number of people.)*

(d) People = 1.8 × 10¹⁰ ÷ 9.125 × 10⁵ = 19,700, about 2.0 × 10⁴ people, which is 100 times the number in part (c). *(1 point)*

(e) According to the second law of thermodynamics, energy transfers are never 100% efficient; at each trophic level much of the energy is lost as heat in metabolism. Eating beef is two transfers beyond the plants, so only about 1% of the original energy reaches people, rather than all of it. *(1 point: heat loss at each transfer linked to fewer people.)*

(f) Much of plant NPP cannot be eaten or digested by people, for example roots, stems, leaves and cellulose; some energy is also needed to run the farm and is lost in processing. *(1 point: gives a valid reason.)*

(g) For example: shift part of the land from cattle to crops people can eat directly, or reduce meat in the diet; or use aquaculture or poultry, which convert feed energy more efficiently. Justification: feeding people at a lower trophic level avoids energy losses at the intermediate steps, so more people can be supported per hectare. *(1 point: proposal; 1 point: justification with trophic levels.)*
:::

:::sol QB-U1-FRQ004
(a) Sea urchin density is higher in the fished area (22 per m², about 7 times the 3 per m² in the reserve), whereas kelp cover is much lower in the fished area (9% compared with 82%). *(1 point: describes both with direction.)*

(b) Sea urchins are primary consumers (second trophic level); large predatory fish that eat urchins are secondary consumers (third trophic level). *(1 point: both levels correct.)*

(c) Fishing removes the large predatory fish, so fewer urchins are eaten and urchin numbers increase. A larger number of urchins graze more kelp, so kelp cover declines. *(1 point: fewer predators lead to more urchins; 1 point: more urchins lead to less kelp.)*

(d) Decrease = (82 − 9) ÷ 82 × 100 = 89% (89.0%). *(1 point)*

(e) The data come from two sites compared once and are only correlational; other differences between the sites (wave exposure, depth, water temperature, nutrients, disease) could explain the difference, and the sites may have differed before the reserve was created. Strengthening: survey the same reefs before and after protection, or use many reserve and fished sites, or run an experiment that excludes urchins or predators from plots. *(1 point: identifies a confounding variable or lack of replication; 1 point: describes one improved design.)*

(f) Kelp cover will increase, because fewer urchins means less grazing, allowing kelp to recover (growth is rapid under favorable light and nutrients). *(1 point: predicts increase with explanation.)*

(g) For example: establish a no-fishing zone or catch limits for the predatory fish so their numbers rebuild and control urchins; or remove urchins manually while the fish recover. Justification: restoring the predator reduces grazing pressure and allows kelp to recover. *(1 point: reasonable action; 1 point: justification via the food web.)*
:::

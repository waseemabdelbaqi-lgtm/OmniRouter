:::sol QB-C1-MCQ004
**Answer: D.** The mantle extends from the base of the crust to about 2,900 km deep and holds about 84% of Earth's volume. The crust (A) is a thin skin, and the two core layers (B, C) together are only about 16% of the volume, even though the core is dense and heavy.

:::sol QB-C1-MCQ006
**Answer: C.** As oceanic lithosphere moves away from a ridge it cools, thickens and becomes denser, so the old Plate Q sinks beneath the young, buoyant Plate P. A is the common misconception that young plates are weak; D is wrong because ocean–ocean convergence produces subduction and volcanic island arcs.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ001
**Answer: D.** Subduction carries water-rich oceanic crust into the mantle, where melting produces magma that rises to build volcanoes on the overriding plate; when the overriding plate is oceanic, the volcanoes form an island arc. A rift valley and a mid-ocean ridge are features of divergent boundaries, where plates move apart, and a transform fault forms where plates slide past each other. The tempting rift-valley option confuses the collision with a divergent setting.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ002
**Answer: D.** A locked fault resists motion while the plates continue to move, so elastic strain accumulates. When the stress overcomes the friction holding the fault, the rocks suddenly slip and release the stored energy as seismic waves. Plates do not stop moving, and smooth continuous slip releases energy gradually instead of producing a sudden earthquake. Volcanic activity can cause small tremors but is not the cause of fault earthquakes.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ014
**Answer: B.** At Boundary II the arrows show Plates Q and R moving toward each other, which is a convergent boundary. Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so Plate Q subducts beneath Plate R, forming a trench offshore and a volcanic mountain range with earthquakes on the continent. Reversing the subduction direction ignores the density difference. Boundary I is divergent and Boundary III is transform, which are the settings for the other two descriptions.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ015
**Answer: C.** Earthquakes occur in the brittle subducting slab as it descends, so foci are shallow near the trench and become deeper farther inland, tracing a plane that dips into the mantle. A vertical plume or a vertical transform fault would not produce a steady increase of focal depth with horizontal distance, and ridge push at a divergent boundary produces only shallow earthquakes.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ037

Collision zones are convergent, not transform or divergent, and an extended subduction process would eventually produce volcanoes.

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ011
**Answer: C.** The tilt of Earth's axis (about 23.5°) points the Northern Hemisphere toward the Sun for part of the orbit, which makes the Sun's rays more direct and lengthens daylight, so insolation increases. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, so distance does not explain the seasons. The Sun's output is nearly constant over a year, and rotation speed does not change with season.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ030
**Answer: D.** At the Northern Hemisphere summer solstice the North Pole is tilted toward the Sun, giving the hemisphere its most direct rays and longest days. At position Z the axis leans toward the Sun, and the Northern Hemisphere is mostly in the lit half. At X the axis leans away (Northern Hemisphere winter solstice), and W and Y are the equinoxes, when the axis is tilted neither toward nor away from the Sun.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ031
**Answer: C.** Near the equator the Sun is nearly overhead, so a beam of sunlight covers a small area; at 60° N the beam arrives at a lower angle, so the same energy is spread over a larger area and travels through a longer path in the atmosphere. Differences in distance from the Sun are negligible, and at the equinox every latitude has about 12 hours of daylight. Air temperature does not determine how much radiation arrives.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ032
**Answer: B.** The tilt of Earth's axis changes day length little near the equator and more at higher latitudes, so P (about 12 hours all year) is nearest the equator. City R has its longest days in December and shortest in June, which is the pattern of the Southern Hemisphere, where December is summer. City Q has long days in June and short days in December, which fits a Northern Hemisphere mid-latitude city.

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ041

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ003
**Answer: A.** Parent material is the original rock or sediment (for example bedrock, glacial deposits, or river-deposited material) that is weathered, and sometimes transported and deposited, to supply the mineral part of a soil. The surface layer of humus is organic matter produced by decomposition, not parent material. Dissolved nutrients are products of weathering and decay, and unweathered bedrock alone is only one possible source, since transported sediment also serves as parent material.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ004
**Answer: C.** Soil formation is faster where warm temperatures and abundant water speed chemical weathering of parent material and the growth and decomposition of organisms that add organic matter. Cold or dry conditions slow these processes. The cold, wet site has water but low temperatures and long freezing periods that slow chemical reactions and biological activity, so it forms soil more slowly than the warm, humid site.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ005
**Answer: B.** As water percolates, soil particles trap suspended material, clay and organic matter adsorb many contaminants, and microbes break down some of them, which cleans the water. The ability is limited: soils can be overwhelmed by large pollutant loads, and they do not stop water from recharging groundwater. More runoff would carry pollutants to streams instead of filtering them.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ006
**Answer: A.** Permeability is the rate at which water moves through a soil, and it depends on the size and connectedness of the pores. Sand has the largest particles and therefore the largest pores, so water drains quickly. Clay has very small pores that slow water movement even though they can hold a lot of water, silt is intermediate, and loam, a blend of all three, drains more slowly than sand.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ016
**Answer: A.** The B horizon is the zone of accumulation: clay and minerals leached from the upper horizons collect there, so it is rich in clay and low in organic matter, which matches Y (36% clay, 1.2% organic matter). W is the organic-rich surface layer, X is the topsoil (A horizon) mixing humus and mineral particles, and Z, with rock fragments and lower clay, is the weathered parent material (C horizon).
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ017
**Answer: D.** Sample 3 is 60% clay. Clay's tiny pores give it a large total surface area and pore volume that hold water by adhesion, but the pores are so small that water moves through slowly, so water-holding capacity is high and permeability is low. Sample 1 is sandy: it drains quickly and holds little water. Sample 2 (40/40/20) is a loam, with intermediate properties, and silt in Sample 4 has larger particles than clay.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ018
**Answer: D.** Porosity is the fraction of the soil volume that is pore space, while permeability depends on how large and how connected the pores are. Clay particles are the smallest of the soil particles, so clay has many very small pores that hold water tightly and offer high resistance to flow. The statement that clay particles are larger than sand is false, and clay actually has the greater water-holding capacity.
:::

The tempting errors are to read the drained volume as the retained volume, or to conclude that adding equal volumes makes the soils equal; the same input shows different results because the soils differ in particle size and pore space.

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ021

### Section B — FRQ Solutions

:::sol QB-C1-FRQ001
(a) Focal depth increases with increasing distance inland from the trench (foci are shallow near the trench and about 160–175 km deep 280–290 km inland). *(1 point: depth increases with distance inland)*

(b) A convergent boundary, specifically a subduction zone (oceanic plate sinking under the continental plate). *(1 point: convergent or subduction)*

(c) Slope = (160 − 12) km ÷ (280 − 40) km = 148 ÷ 240 = 0.62 (about 0.6 km of depth per km of distance; a dip of about 32°). *(1 point: correct substitution of change in depth over change in distance; 1 point: answer of about 0.6 or 0.62, units km/km acceptable)*

(d) The sinking plate carries water that is released as the plate reaches about 100 km depth (the slab is about 110 km deep below the volcanoes). The water lowers the melting point of the hot mantle above the slab, producing magma that is less dense than the surrounding rock and rises to form volcanoes. At the trench the slab is too shallow and too cool for this melting to occur. *(1 point: water released from the slab/slab at about 100 km depth; 1 point: lowers the melting point of the mantle so magma forms and rises)* Accept: melting of the slab itself at depth.

(e) Difference in magnitude = 6.8 − 5.6 = 1.2; energy ratio = 31.6^1.2 = 10^(1.5 × 1.2) = 10^1.8 ≈ 63 times more energy. *(1 point: about 63; accept 60–65)*

(f) Claim: for the same magnitude, a shallow earthquake causes stronger shaking at the surface than a deep one. Evidence: Earthquake X (15 km deep) produced intensity IX, but Earthquake Y (105 km deep) produced only VI, even though both had magnitude 6.8. Reason: energy from a deep focus travels farther and spreads out and weakens before it reaches the surface. *(1 point: claim that shallow focus gives stronger shaking; 1 point: data comparison of X and Y or the reasoning on distance and attenuation)*

(g) Any one with justification, for example: enforce earthquake-resistant building codes (flexible, reinforced structures) so buildings do not collapse; install early-warning systems and tsunami warning sirens; restrict development on soft sediments and the coastline; or run earthquake drills and mark evacuation routes. Justification must link the action to fewer deaths or injuries. *(1 point: valid action with a justification)*
:::

:::sol QB-U4-FRQ001
(a) Soil erosion from the orchard (loss of topsoil) and the resulting sediment pollution (turbidity) of the stream. *(1 point: soil erosion or sediment runoff/stream sedimentation)*

(b) Example: *If* a grass cover is planted between the tree rows, *then* the mass of sediment lost from the block with grass will be less than from the block with bare soil during the same rain events. *(1 point: testable if–then statement that predicts the direction of the effect of cover on soil loss)*

(c) Independent variable: ground cover between the tree rows (grass versus bare soil). Dependent variable: soil loss, measured as the dry mass of sediment caught in the sediment trap (for example kg per rain event or per hectare); stream turbidity is also acceptable if measured the same way for each block. *(1 point for each variable; the dependent variable must be something measured and match the hypothesis)*

(d) Blocks 1 and 2: they have the same slope (6%) and the same soil (loam), so only the ground cover will differ between them. Slope and soil texture strongly affect erosion, so comparing blocks with different slopes or soils (for example Block 1 with Block 3 or 4) would confound the results. Blocks 3 and 4 have the same slope but different soil, so they are not a valid pair. *(1 point: Blocks 1 and 2; 1 point: reason that slope and soil type are the same, so cover is the only difference)*

(e) Sow grass between the rows in one of the two blocks (for example Block 2) and keep the other (Block 1) bare as the control. Install identical sediment traps at the downhill edge of each block and a rain gauge at the site. After each rain event, collect the sediment from each trap, dry it in an oven, and weigh it, and record the rainfall amount. Repeat for many storms (or one full rainy season, ideally for more than one year), and, if possible, use more than one trap per block, so that the results are reliable. *(1 point: grass in one block with the other as bare control and sediment traps with mass of dry sediment measured; 1 point: replication such as many storms or traps, with rainfall recorded)*

(f) Support: the sediment mass from the grass block is consistently lower than from the bare block across storms, by an amount larger than the storm-to-storm variation (for example confirmed by a statistical test such as a t-test), and the difference holds when rainfall amount is similar. *(1 point: lower soil loss in the grass block than in the bare block, consistently across rain events)*

(g) Any one with a justification, for example: planting rows along the contour (contour farming), which slows runoff and increases infiltration; terracing steeper blocks (3 and 4), which shortens the slope length; adding mulch or leaving crop residue, which protects the soil from raindrop impact; planting a riparian buffer strip of vegetation between the orchard and the stream, which traps sediment before it reaches the water. *(1 point: valid practice with justification linked to reducing erosion or sediment reaching the stream)*
:::

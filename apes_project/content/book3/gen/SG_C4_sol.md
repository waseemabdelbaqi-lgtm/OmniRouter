**Answer: (a) resource partitioning; (b) it separates the species' use of the seeds so they rarely compete; (c) competition increases.**

(a) The four species feed at different plant heights (0–10, 10–30, 30–60 and 60–100 cm), so they share the seed supply by **resource partitioning** (here, spatial partitioning).

(b) Each species takes seeds from a different layer, so a seed eaten by one species is not one that another species needed. Overlap in resource use is small, so interspecific competition is weak and each species keeps enough food to survive and reproduce.

(c) **Competition increases.** Half as many seeds means each bird must find food from a smaller supply, so individuals within each species (intraspecific) and, where feeding zones touch, between species compete harder for what is left. A common wrong answer says competition falls because "there is less food"; scarcity makes interactions stronger, not weaker.

**Answer:** a model design is below. Other reasonable designs are acceptable if every element is present.

(a) **Hypothesis:** If the pH of the water is lowered below a certain level, then the percent survival of salamander larvae will decrease, because the acidity exceeds the larvae's range of tolerance.

(b) **Independent variable:** pH of the water. **Dependent variable:** percent survival of the larvae after a set time (growth or deformities could be added).

(c) **Treatments:** use a series of graded pH levels, for example 7.0, 6.5, 6.0, 5.5, 5.0, 4.5, and 4.0, made by adding dilute acid to pond water. **Control:** the natural pH of the pond (about 7.0), with no acid added. **Replication:** at least 3 to 5 identical containers at each pH, each with the same number of larvae (for example, 20), so that chance differences between individuals average out.

(d) **Constants** (any two): water temperature, water volume, food type and amount, light and day length, age and size of larvae, source (same pond or clutch) of larvae, duration of the experiment, container type.

(e) After the set time (for example, 14 days) count survivors in each container and calculate percent survival for each pH, using the mean of the replicates. Graph survival (y-axis) against pH (x-axis). The tolerance limit is the pH where survival drops sharply toward zero; the range of tolerance is the span between the upper and lower limits. Survival that is reduced but above zero marks the zone of stress.

:::sol SG-U1-E010
**Answer: (a) grass → grasshoppers → lizards → hawks; the hawk is a tertiary consumer; (b) grasshoppers increase; (c) negative feedback.**

(a) The longest chain is **grass → grasshoppers → lizards → hawks** (four levels). Grass is the producer (level 1), grasshoppers are primary consumers (level 2), lizards are secondary consumers (level 3) and the hawk is a **tertiary consumer** (level 4) in this chain. (In the shorter chain grass → rabbits → hawks the hawk is a secondary consumer, so it occupies more than one level in the web.)

(b) The grasshopper population **increases**. Lizards are their only predator, so removing the lizards removes the main source of mortality; grasshoppers survive and reproduce more, limited only by food or other factors. (Hawks would then rely more on rabbits, which can affect the rabbits as well.)

(c) This is **negative (balancing) feedback**. A rise in rabbits increases the foxes' food and fox numbers, and more foxes then eat more rabbits, which reverses the original rise and pushes rabbits back down, stabilizing the system.

(a) Station A (25 °C, 300 cm/yr) is hot and very wet: **tropical rainforest**. Station B (−9 °C, 22 cm/yr) is very cold with low precipitation: **tundra**. Station C (18 °C, 12 cm/yr) has precipitation under about 30 cm/yr: **desert**, even though it is not especially hot.

:::sol SG-U1-E011
**Answer: (a) M = savanna, N = temperate grassland; (b) the dry season selects for drought-tolerant, fire-adapted plants; (c) N would shift toward shrubland or desert.**

(a) **Station M is a savanna.** Evidence: (1) temperature is warm all year (24–28 °C, almost no seasonal change); (2) rainfall is strongly seasonal, with a wet season (150–230 mm/month, November–April) and a long dry season (under 15 mm/month, May–October). A rainforest would have rain in every month.
**Station N is a temperate grassland.** Evidence: (1) a cold winter (−6 °C in January) and a warm summer (20 °C in July), a large seasonal temperature range; (2) low-to-moderate precipitation (15–25 mm in winter), with rainfall peaking in summer (60–90 mm, May–August). Too little rain for forest, too much for desert.

(b) Plants in the savanna must survive six months with almost no rain. Grasses die back or go dormant in the dry season and regrow when rains return, trees are scattered and often drop their leaves or have deep roots, and dry grass fuels fires, so the plants that dominate are drought-tolerant and fire-adapted; the dry season also limits tree density so grasses dominate.

(c) If the region warmed and became drier, evaporation would rise and soil moisture would fall, so tall grasses would be replaced by more drought-tolerant shrubs and short grasses. The biome would move toward **shrubland or, if precipitation fell low enough (under about 30 cm/yr), desert**, because biomes are determined by temperature and precipitation. Station N's biome boundary would shift accordingly.

:::sol SG-U2-E006
**Answer: (a) population bottleneck; genetic diversity decreased; (b) fewer alleles means less chance of resistance; (c) behavior is individual, adaptation is a population change.**

(a) The collapse from 2,000 to 40 mice was a **population bottleneck**. Only the survivors passed on genes, and rare alleles were lost by chance. Five alleles fell to two, a loss of (2 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = 60% of the alleles. Genetic diversity is therefore **lower** even though the population size is back to 2,000; regrowing in number does not restore lost genes.

(b) With only two immune-gene alleles, the chance that any mouse carries a form that resists the new virulent disease is much smaller than in the original population, which had five. If none carries resistance, nearly all could die. The original population had more variation, so more individuals were likely to survive and rebuild it.

(c) A mouse that changes its behavior (for example, avoiding sick mice) is making an individual, within-lifetime response that is not inherited. Adaptation is a change in the population across generations: mice with heritable traits that improve survival leave more offspring, so those traits (such as a resistant allele) become more common. Behavior counts as adaptation only if it is inherited.
:::

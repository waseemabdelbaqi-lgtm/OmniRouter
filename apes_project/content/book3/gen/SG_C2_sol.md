## Chapter 2 — The Atmosphere

:::sol SG-C2-E001
**(a) 420 ppm. (b) 0.101 m³. (c) 187.4 m³. (d) 50%. (e) Water vapor.**
(a) ppm = percent × 10,000 = 0.0420 × 10,000 = 420 ppm.
(b) Volume = 240 m³ × (0.0420 ÷ 100) = 240 × 0.000420 = 0.1008 m³ ≈ 0.101 m³.
(c) Volume = 240 m³ × (78.08 ÷ 100) = 240 × 0.7808 = 187.39 m³ ≈ 187.4 m³.
(d) Percent increase = (420 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = 140 ÷ 280 × 100 = 50%.
(e) Water vapor varies from near 0% to about 4% with temperature and place; N₂ and CO₂ are nearly constant everywhere.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E005
**Answer: (a) 6.5°C per km of cooling. (b) Troposphere; stratosphere. (c) Ozone absorbs UV. (d) About 12 km.**

(a) Average rate = (T lower − T higher) ÷ altitude difference = (18 − (−60)) ÷ (12 − 0) = 78°C ÷ 12 km = **6.5°C per km** (the temperature falls with height, as expected for the average lapse rate).

(b) The 0–12 km layer, where temperature falls with altitude, is the **troposphere**. The 16–50 km layer, where temperature rises from −60°C to −3°C, is the **stratosphere**.

(c) The trend reverses because the stratosphere contains the **ozone** layer. Ozone (O₃) absorbs ultraviolet radiation from the sun, which warms the air, so temperature rises with altitude instead of falling. The troposphere, in contrast, is heated from the ground below, so the air farthest from the surface is coldest.

(d) The tropopause is at about **12 km**, where the temperature stops falling and reaches its minimum (−60°C). The data show it staying at −60°C up to 16 km, so the turning point lies in the 12–16 km range, and 12 km is the first altitude at which the cooling trend ends.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E009
**Answer: (a) 4.4 times. (b) City N. (c) Rising, cooling air drops its moisture on the windward side. (d) Drier vegetation at N.**

(a) 1,850 mm ÷ 420 mm = 4.405, so City M receives about **4.4 times** as much precipitation as City N.

(b) **City N** is in the rain shadow. The prevailing winds blow from the west, so the western foot (City M) is the windward side and the eastern foot (City N) is the leeward side.

(c) Moist westerly air meets the mountains and is forced to rise. As it rises it expands and cools, so water vapor condenses into clouds and falls as heavy rain or snow on the windward slope. The air, now much drier, crosses the crest, descends on the leeward side, is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and little rain falls. The result is a dry region downwind (the rain shadow).

(d) City N, with only 420 mm a year, would have drought-tolerant vegetation such as grassland, sagebrush or desert scrub, while wet City M would support lush forest. A coastal city has a smaller annual temperature range because water has a high specific heat: the ocean heats and cools slowly and moderates the air temperature, whereas inland air over land heats and cools more.

:::sol SG-U4-E006
**Answer: (a) The northeast trade winds, blowing from the northeast. (b) The Coriolis effect deflects the air to the right. (c) Toward the east, carried by the prevailing westerlies. (d) Sinking dry air at 30° N.**

(a) At 15° N the ship is in the trade-wind belt (0°–30°). The prevailing wind there is the **northeast trade wind**, which blows *from* the northeast, toward the southwest, helping a ship cross from West Africa to the Caribbean.

(b) Surface air in the Hadley cell flows toward the equator, which by itself would be due south. Earth rotates eastward, and the air keeps the greater eastward speed of the higher latitude it left, so relative to the ground it appears to curve **to the right** in the Northern Hemisphere. A southward-moving flow turned to the right ends up moving toward the southwest, so the wind comes from the northeast instead of directly from the north.

(c) At 45° N the storm is in the **prevailing westerlies** belt (30°–60°), so it is most likely carried **west to east** (toward the east).

(d) Air that rose at the equator loses its moisture as rain, moves poleward aloft, cools, and sinks near 30°. Sinking air creates high pressure, warms as it descends and becomes drier, so clouds and rain are rare. This produces the Sahara and other deserts near 30° N.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E010
**Answer: (a) Year 1 El Niño, Year 2 neutral, Year 3 La Niña, Year 4 neutral. (b) −56%. (c) Upwelling collapses in El Niño. (d) Drier in Year 1, wetter in Year 3.**

(a) Using the rule: Year 1 (+2.1°C ≥ +0.5) is **El Niño**; Year 2 (−0.3°C, between −0.5 and +0.5) is **neutral**; Year 3 (−1.6°C ≤ −0.5) is **La Niña**; Year 4 (+0.2°C) is **neutral**.

(b) Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 = (4 − 9) ÷ 9 × 100 = −55.6%, so the catch **fell by about 56%** from Year 2 to Year 1.

(c) In the El Niño year (Year 1) the trade winds weaken, so warm surface water spreads east toward South America and the thermocline deepens. Upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water off Peru weakens or stops, so nutrients are not brought to the surface and phytoplankton decline. Anchovies, which feed on plankton, decline, and the catch is lowest (4 million tonnes).

(d) In Year 1 (El Niño) the rain shifts east, so Indonesia has **drought** (less rain, with greater wildfire risk). In Year 3 (La Niña) the strengthened trade winds pile warm water in the west and rain concentrates there, so Indonesia has **heavier than normal rain and flooding**.
:::

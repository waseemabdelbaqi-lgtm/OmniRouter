### Section A — MCQ Answers and Explanations

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ008
**Answer: D.** The layers are defined by temperature gradients: temperature falls with altitude in the troposphere, rises in the stratosphere, falls again in the mesosphere, and rises in the thermosphere. The proportions of nitrogen and oxygen are nearly constant in the lower layers, so composition does not define them. Air pressure decreases gradually and never abruptly reaches zero, and water vapor is concentrated mainly in the troposphere but does not set the boundaries.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ022
**Answer: B.** In layer X temperature rises from about −56°C to about −2°C as altitude increases from 12 km to 50 km, because ozone concentrated in this layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation and warms the surrounding air. In W and Y temperature falls with altitude, and in Z temperature rises because molecules there absorb very high-energy solar radiation, not because of the ozone layer.

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ039
**Answer: A.** In the stratosphere, warm air lies above cooler air because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation, which makes the layer stable and resistant to vertical mixing; the air also contains very little water, so rain and snow do not wash particles out. The troposphere, in contrast, mixes strongly and has frequent precipitation. The stratosphere has little water vapor, little convection, and gravity there is almost the same as at Earth's surface.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ012

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ033
**Answer: D.** Precipitation increases from the coast (W, 900 mm) to the windward slope (X, 2,300 mm) and then drops sharply behind the crest (Z, 350 mm). That pattern shows orographic lifting and cooling of moist air on the windward side, followed by descending, warming, drier air on the leeward side. A steady decline with distance from the ocean would not show X wetter than W. Elevation alone does not explain Z, and air rises, not sinks, on the windward slope.

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ034
**Answer: D.** Water has a high heat capacity, so the ocean warms slowly in summer and cools slowly in winter, and marine air blowing onshore keeps coastal temperatures from changing as much over the year. Inland cities respond quickly to changes in insolation and show large seasonal ranges. Ocean air is not always warmer than land air, and distance from the ocean does not change the intensity of insolation at a given latitude.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ042
**Answer: C.** At 45° N the prevailing winds are the westerlies, which blow from west to east. City P receives air that has passed over the ocean, which keeps winters mild and summers cool, while City Q receives air that has traveled over the land mass, which cools strongly in winter and heats in summer. Both cities are at the same latitude, trade winds blow at lower latitudes, and a rain shadow affects precipitation more than temperature range.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ009
**Answer: A.** Because Earth rotates, the surface moves at different speeds at different latitudes, and moving air appears to be deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Differences in land and ocean heating cause local pressure differences, and the tilt of the axis causes the seasons, but neither deflects moving air in this way.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ023
**Answer: C.** The arrows at X show air sinking. Descending air is compressed and warms, so its relative humidity falls and clouds dissipate, which creates the dry conditions of many deserts near 30° latitude. Rising air at the equator (W) and near 60° (Y) cools, condenses, and produces rain. Cold air at the surface and ocean winds do not explain the dryness at X.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ024
**Answer: B.** Between the equator and about 30° N, surface air flows toward the equator (south). Earth's rotation deflects this moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, so it moves toward the southwest, which means the wind blows from the northeast. These are the northeast trade winds. Winds from the southeast occur in the Southern Hemisphere, where deflection is to the left.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ025
**Answer: D.** The most intense insolation arrives at the equator, heating the surface and the air above it. Warm, moist air is less dense and rises; as it rises it cools, water vapor condenses, and heavy rain falls. Sinking air is dry, the Coriolis effect is weakest (zero) at the equator, and mountains do not circle the equator.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ040
**Answer: D.** Without rotation there would be no Coriolis effect, so moving air would not be deflected. Air heated at the equator would rise and move poleward aloft, cool, sink near the pole, and return along the surface toward the equator, so surface winds would blow directly from north to south in the Northern Hemisphere. The current three-cell pattern with east–west components depends on rotation, and heating would still be uneven.
:::

:::sol QB-C2-MCQ004
**Answer: D.** Warm surface water (about 26.5°C or higher over a large area) supplies the heat and moisture that fuel a tropical cyclone. The Coriolis effect is needed to start rotation, but it is zero at the equator (C), so storms form at least about 5° from it, and cold water or land (A, B) starves the storm.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ013
**Answer: C.** El Niño is defined by warmer-than-average surface water across the central and eastern tropical Pacific. Unusually cold water in that region marks La Niña, the opposite phase. El Niño is a Pacific phenomenon, and the warm water shifts toward the east rather than staying only in the western Pacific.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ035
**Answer: A.** A +2.3°C anomaly indicates an El Niño. Weaker trade winds let warm surface water spread east and deepen, so upwelling brings little nutrient-rich cold water to the surface, phytoplankton decline, and the food web supporting anchovies collapses. Warm water holds less, not more, dissolved oxygen, El Niño weakens (not strengthens) the trade winds, and the water in Year 3 was warmer, not colder.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ043
**Answer: B.** La Niña features unusually cold surface water in the eastern tropical Pacific, strong trade winds that pile warm water in the west, heavy rain over Indonesia, and dry conditions along the Peruvian coast. Year X matches all three (−1.6°C, 35% of normal Peruvian rainfall, 135% in Indonesia). Year W shows the opposite, a strong El Niño, Year Y is near normal, and Year Z is a weak warm event.
:::

:::sol QB-U4-MCQ044
**Answer: C.** During El Niño the trade winds weaken, so the warm surface water that normally accumulates in the western Pacific moves east. Warm water deepens the warm surface layer off South America, so upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water declines, and rising air and heavy rain move toward the central and eastern Pacific. The sequence that begins with strengthening trade winds describes La Niña, and the remaining sequences reverse or contradict the cause-and-effect order.
:::

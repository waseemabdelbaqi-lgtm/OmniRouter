:::sol SG-U4-E001
**Answer: (a) Site 1 convergent (oceanic–continental subduction); Site 2 divergent; Site 3 transform. (b) The plates move apart and new crust forms by seafloor spreading. (c) Site 1.**

(a) Site 1: a deep trench offshore with a line of volcanoes inland shows an oceanic plate sinking beneath a continental plate, so the boundary is convergent. Site 2: a mid-ocean ridge with new basalt and seafloor that grows older on both sides is a divergent boundary. Site 3: a road offset sideways with no volcanoes shows plates sliding horizontally past each other, a transform boundary (like the San Andreas Fault).

(b) At Site 2 the two plates move apart. Magma rises into the gap, cools and hardens into new basalt crust, and the older crust is carried away on both sides. This process of adding new crust is **seafloor spreading**.

(c) Site 1 is the most likely source of a tsunami, because a large subduction-zone earthquake can suddenly lift or drop the seafloor and displace the water above it. A transform fault slides sideways with little vertical displacement, and ridge earthquakes are usually small and shallow.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E016
**Answer: (a) 61.5° in June, 14.5° in December. (b) About 3.5 times. (c) 117 W/m². (d) Smaller changes in sun angle and day length near the equator.**

(a) Noon sun angle = 90° − |latitude − latitude of the direct sun|.
June: 90° − |52° − 23.5°| = 90° − 28.5° = **61.5°**.
December: 90° − |52° − (−23.5°)| = 90° − 75.5° = **14.5°**.

(b) Intensity is proportional to the sine of the noon angle. sin 61.5° ≈ 0.879 and sin 14.5° ≈ 0.250. Ratio = 0.879 ÷ 0.250 ≈ **3.5**, so June noon sunlight is about 3.5 times as intense as December's.

(c) Energy absorbed = incoming × (1 − albedo). Snow: 180 × (1 − 0.80) = 36 W/m². Bare soil: 180 × (1 − 0.15) = 153 W/m². Increase = 153 − 36 = **117 W/m²** (check: 180 × (0.80 − 0.15) = 117 W/m²).

(d) A city at 5° N is near the equator, so its noon sun angle stays high all year (about 71.5° in June and 61.5° in December), and its day length stays near 12 hours. Intensity and hours of daylight change little, so temperatures change little. At 52° N the noon sun angle drops from 61.5° to 14.5° and daylight changes greatly between summer and winter, causing large seasonal temperature swings.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E008
**Answer: (a) 73.5° in June, 26.5° in December. (b) June. (c) Longer daylight in June. (d) The equator (0°).**

(a) Noon sun angle = 90° − |latitude − latitude of the direct sun|.
June solstice (direct sun at +23.5°): 90° − |40° − 23.5°| = 90° − 16.5° = **73.5°**.
December solstice (direct sun at −23.5°): 90° − |40° − (−23.5°)| = 90° − 63.5° = **26.5°**.

(b) The **June solstice** gives more intense radiation per unit area. The higher sun angle (73.5°) concentrates the same beam of sunlight on a smaller area of ground, and the rays travel a shorter path through the atmosphere, so less is absorbed, scattered or reflected. In December the low sun spreads the energy over a larger area and passes through more air. (The sines make the point: sin 73.5° ≈ 0.96 and sin 26.5° ≈ 0.45, so June is about 2.1 times as intense.)

(c) Day length: in June the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the sun, so daylight lasts longer (about 15 hours at 40° N) than in December (about 9 hours), giving more hours of exposure.

(d) On the March equinox the sun is directly overhead at the **equator (0° latitude)**.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E012
**Answer: (a) 11 t/ha/yr. (b) 8,250 t. (c) 0.96 mm per year. (d) Not sustainable.**

(a) Net loss = erosion − formation = 12 − 1.0 = **11 t/ha/yr**.

(b) Total = net rate × area × time = 11 t/ha/yr × 25 ha × 30 yr = **8,250 t** (about 8.3 × 10³ t).

(c) Depth lost = mass lost ÷ (bulk density × area). Per hectare per year: 12 t ÷ (1.25 t/m³ × 10⁴ m²) = 12 ÷ 12,500 = 9.6 × 10⁻⁴ m = **0.96 mm per year**.

(d) **Not sustainable.** Soil is lost at 0.96 mm per year but forms at only about 0.08 mm per year, so loss is about 12 times faster than formation, and the topsoil is steadily depleted. (The formation rate agrees with 1.0 t/ha/yr: 1.0 ÷ 12,500 = 0.08 mm.) One practice that would move erosion toward the formation rate: no-till farming, cover crops, contour plowing, terracing, strip cropping or a vegetated buffer strip, each of which keeps the surface covered, slows runoff or wind and binds the soil with roots.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E002
**Answer: (a) P = O, Q = A, R = E, S = B, T = C, U = R. (b) Leaching removes material from R and deposits it in S. (c) Q (the A horizon, topsoil).**

(a) P is loose, partly decayed litter, so it is the **O** horizon. Q is dark, humus-rich and root-filled, so it is the **A** horizon. R is pale, sandy and nutrient-poor, so it is the **E** horizon. S is reddish and clay-rich, so it is the **B** horizon. T is broken, partly weathered parent material, so it is the **C** horizon. U is solid, unbroken bedrock, so it is the **R** horizon.

(b) Water percolating down through R carries away (leaches) fine clay, dissolved minerals, iron and humus, leaving behind the pale, coarse sand grains that hold few nutrients. The material removed from R is carried down and deposited in S, where it accumulates, which is why S is clay-rich and reddish.

(c) The farmer loses the topsoil, the dark, humus-rich A horizon (Q). On a bare field there is no litter layer to protect it, and if the litter (P) is present it is stripped first. Practices that reduce the loss include cover crops, no-till farming, contour plowing, terracing, strip cropping or windbreaks, which keep the surface covered, slow water or wind, and bind soil with roots.
:::

:::sol SG-U4-E003
**Answer: (a) sand 42%, silt 36%, clay 22%. (b) Loam. (c) Clay.**

(a) Percent = component mass ÷ total mass × 100.
Sand = 21.0 g ÷ 50.0 g × 100 = **42%**.
Silt = 18.0 g ÷ 50.0 g × 100 = **36%**.
Clay = 11.0 g ÷ 50.0 g × 100 = **22%**. These sum to 100%.

(b) Check the simplified key in order. Sand ≥ 85%? No (42%). Clay ≥ 40%? No (22%). Silt ≥ 80%? No (36%). Loam requires sand 23–52%, silt 28–50% and clay 7–27%: 42% sand, 36% silt and 22% clay all fall in range, so the texture class is **loam**.

(c) Clay holds the most water against gravity. Its particles are the smallest, so its pores are very tiny and have a huge total surface area; water is held tightly on the particle surfaces and in the narrow pores instead of draining out under gravity.
:::

**Answer: (a) 112 cattle, 40% above the sustainable number. (b) Each rancher keeps the full gain and shares the loss. (c) A grazing quota, for example 10 cattle per rancher.**

(a) Total cattle = 8 ranchers × 14 cattle = 112 cattle. Excess = 112 − 80 = 32 cattle. Percent over = 32 ÷ 80 × 100% = **40%**.

(b) A meadow open to all is a commons. An extra animal gives its owner the whole sale value, but the cost of lost grass is divided among all eight ranchers, so each rancher bears only about 1/8 of it. Adding an animal is therefore rational for each rancher even though everyone knows the meadow is overgrazed. If all eight add animals, the grass cover fails and every rancher loses. A common wrong answer blames greed or ignorance; the cause is self-interest combined with no enforceable limit.

(c) Rule: a permit system limiting the meadow to 80 cattle in total, for example 10 per rancher (8 × 10 = 80), enforced by inspection and fines. Drawbacks (any one): it costs money to monitor and enforce; an equal share may be unfair to ranchers with larger families or businesses; or it reduces each rancher's income in the short term. Privatizing the meadow in equal plots would be another acceptable rule, with the drawback that it could favor wealthier buyers.

:::sol SG-U5-E002
**Answer:** Cutting the trees removes shade, root-held soil and canopy interception, which raises stream temperature, sediment and peak flow. The clearcut also releases carbon as CO₂, and it is cheap and fast.

(a) *Temperature:* with no canopy, sunlight strikes the stream and the ground directly, so water warms (14 °C to 21 °C). Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, which stresses cold-water fish. *Sediment:* roots no longer hold the soil and rain hits bare ground, so erosion increases and eroded soil washes into the stream. *Peak flow:* with no canopy to intercept rain and no roots drawing water out of the soil, less water infiltrates or is taken up. More water runs off quickly over the surface, so the storm peak is higher and flooding downstream is more likely.

(b) The stored carbon in the cut trees is released as CO₂ when the wood is burned or decays, which adds to the greenhouse effect. The loss of the trees also removes a carbon sink, so less CO₂ is absorbed in future years. Either effect earns credit.

(c) Clearcutting is the cheapest and fastest harvest method. Machines can reach every tree, roads are simple, and the cleared land can be replanted with one fast-growing species. Freeing the land for crops, pasture or buildings is another acceptable advantage.
:::

**Answer: (a) 4%, 8% and 16%. (b) Humans apply the pesticide that decides which moths survive. (c) Use IPM or refuge areas.**

(a) Generation 1 = 2%. Doubling each generation: generation 2 = 2 × 2% = **4%**; generation 3 = **8%**; generation 4 = **16%**. Resistance rose eightfold in three generations.

(b) Individual moths vary genetically, and some already carry a resistance allele. The insecticide, applied by the grower, kills most susceptible moths while the resistant ones survive and reproduce, so the resistant share rises each generation. Selection is driven by human action, hence *artificial* selection. The moths did not "learn" or change; the existing variation was sorted.

(c) Proposal (any one): use integrated pest management, spraying only when the moth count passes an economic threshold and using biocontrol such as parasitic wasps; leave an untreated refuge so susceptible moths keep breeding with resistant ones; or rotate insecticides with different modes of action. Drawbacks: IPM needs training, careful field scouting and sometimes more labor, and its results are slower than a quick spray. A refuge sacrifices some crop to pest damage.

:::sol SG-U5-E007
**Answer: (a) 100 thousand t per year at a stock of 500 thousand t. (b) −26 thousand t. (c) The stock keeps falling and collapses.**

(a) Maximum natural growth is the largest value in the data: **100 thousand tonnes per year at a stock of 500 thousand tonnes**. This is the maximum sustainable yield.

(b) Growth at 800 = 64; harvest = 90. Change = growth − harvest = 64 − 90 = **−26 thousand tonnes**. The stock falls from 800 to about 774 thousand tonnes after one year. Growth is below the harvest, so the stock declines even though the harvest is below the 100 maximum.

(c) At 200 the growth is only 64, so growth − harvest = 64 − 90 = −26 each year. The stock gets smaller, growth gets smaller still (the curve falls as the stock drops), and the gap widens, so the stock collapses. A harvest of 90 can be sustained only if the stock stays above the unstable point somewhere between 400 and 500 (where growth just equals 90). The fleet's harvest level is the same whatever the stock, so nothing reduces fishing as the stock falls. This is a tragedy of the commons: each boat gains the full catch, the cost of fewer fish is shared by everyone, and without enforceable quotas the stock is fished down until everyone loses. A fix is a quota set below the maximum sustainable yield and lowered when the stock is low.

**Answer: (a) 125,000 t of ore at 0.8%; 250,000 t at 0.4%. (b) 124,000 t and 249,000 t of tailings. (c) Lower grade means more rock per tonne of metal; reduce demand by recycling copper.**

(a) Ore = metal ÷ grade. At 0.8% (0.008): 1,000 ÷ 0.008 = **125,000 t**. At 0.4% (0.004): 1,000 ÷ 0.004 = **250,000 t**.

(b) Tailings = ore − metal. At 0.8%: 125,000 − 1,000 = **124,000 t**. At 0.4%: 250,000 − 1,000 = **249,000 t**.

(c) Halving the grade doubles the rock that must be mined, hauled, crushed and processed for the same 1,000 t of copper. That raises energy, water and chemical costs and doubles the overburden and tailings, so there is more habitat loss, dust and risk of acid mine drainage or leaks from tailings ponds. Reducing demand for newly mined copper: recycle copper wiring and pipes, reuse products, or substitute another material. A drawback of recycling is that collection and sorting cost money and not all copper is recovered.

:::sol SG-U5-E018
**Answer:** a model experiment compares replicated mulched and bare slopes under the same conditions and measures the sediment in the runoff.

(a) *Hypothesis:* If bare construction slopes are covered with a layer of wood-chip mulch, then less sediment will wash into runoff than from bare slopes with no mulch.

(b) *Independent variable:* presence (or thickness) of wood-chip mulch on the slope. *Dependent variable:* mass of sediment in the runoff, in grams (or turbidity in NTU, or sediment per liter of runoff, measured after each storm). *Control group:* slopes with bare soil and no mulch. *Controlled variables (any two):* slope angle, soil type, plot area, rainfall amount or simulated rain, time of measurement, how the runoff is collected, and the starting soil condition.

(c) Use several replicate plots per treatment, for example five mulched and five bare plots of the same size, so that chance differences do not decide the result. Assign each treatment to plots at random so that a sunnier or steeper corner does not line up with one treatment. After each storm, collect all runoff from each plot in a bucket, then dry and weigh the sediment, and compare the treatment mean with the control mean. The hypothesis is supported if the mean sediment from the mulched plots is lower than the control mean by more than the scatter (range or error bars) among the replicates. Weak designs change two variables at once, use a single plot, or compare different sites or years.
:::

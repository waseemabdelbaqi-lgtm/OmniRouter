## Unit 3 — Populations

:::sol SG-U3-E001
**Answer:** (a) Beetle A is a specialist and Beetle B is a generalist; (b) A crashes and may disappear locally while B persists or grows; (c) A would be favored in a completely stable forest.

(a) Beetle A has a narrow niche (one tree species for eggs and food), so it is a **specialist**. Beetle B eats more than twenty tree species and lives in several habitat types, so it is a **generalist**.

(b) Beetle A's only resource has been removed, so it has nowhere to switch. Its population should fall sharply over the five years and could go locally extinct. Beetle B simply feeds on the many other tree species that survive, so its population should hold steady or even grow (it may gain if A's loss reduces competition). A common wrong answer says both decline because "the forest was damaged"; that ignores niche width.

(c) **Beetle A** (the specialist). In an unchanging habitat a specialist is fine-tuned to its one resource and uses it more efficiently than a generalist, so it out-competes the generalist for that resource. With no disturbance, the specialist's weakness never shows.
:::

**Answer:** (a) P is r-selected and Q is K-selected; (b) Q would be harmed more; (c) R has a mix of traits and sits between the extremes.

(a) **P is r-selected**: it is tiny (2 g) and produces 400 eggs per season, matures at only 3 months, and lives about 1 year (any two of these). **Q is K-selected**: it is huge (4,500 kg) and has one calf every 3 years, matures at 12 years and lives 60 years (any two).

(b) **Species Q.** It reproduces so slowly (one calf per 3 years, first breeding at 12 years) that it cannot replace individuals lost to a predator. Species P can produce hundreds of offspring and mature within months, so its population rebounds quickly. A common wrong answer says P is harmed more because it is smaller; body size does not determine recovery, reproductive speed does.

(c) R has traits from both strategies. Its moderate mass (25 kg), moderate maturity age (2 years) and moderate life span (12 years) are intermediate. Two pups per year is a modest number (far fewer than P's 400 eggs, but repeated every year rather than once every three years like Q). r and K are ends of a spectrum, and most species fall between them, so R is best described as having a tendency toward one side rather than as a pure r or pure K species.

:::sol SG-U3-E006
**Answer:** (a) 150, 200 and 38 fish per year; (b) fastest at N = 1,000; (c) −312 fish per year, a decline.

(a) dN/dt = r × N × (K − N) ÷ K, with r = 0.40 per year and K = 2,000 fish.
- N = 500: 0.40 × 500 × (1,500 ÷ 2,000) = 200 × 0.75 = **150 fish per year**.
- N = 1,000: 0.40 × 1,000 × (1,000 ÷ 2,000) = 400 × 0.5 = **200 fish per year**.
- N = 1,900: 0.40 × 1,900 × (100 ÷ 2,000) = 760 × 0.05 = **38 fish per year**.

(b) Growth is fastest at **N = 1,000**, which is K/2 = 2,000 ÷ 2. Growth is slowest at N = 1,900 because the population is almost at K: only 5% of the capacity is still open, so the factor (K − N) ÷ K is tiny. Resources are nearly used up, so the population adds few individuals.

(c) dN/dt = 0.40 × 2,600 × (2,000 − 2,600) ÷ 2,000 = 1,040 × (−0.30) = **−312 fish per year**. The negative sign means the population is above K (overshoot) and is shrinking by about 312 fish per year.

:::sol SG-U3-E014
**Answer:** (a) rabbits peak year 2, foxes peak year 4, a lag of 2 years; (b) see below; (c) 60%; (d) negative feedback, density-dependent.

(a) The rabbit peak is at **year 2** (1,500) and the fox peak at **year 4** (90). The foxes lag the rabbits by **2 years**.

(b) When rabbits are abundant, foxes find plenty of food and raise more young, but fox numbers take time to rise because of the time needed for mating, gestation and young growing to adulthood. Foxes therefore keep increasing even after rabbits have started to decline. As foxes become numerous they eat more rabbits and the rabbit population falls; the food supply then falls and foxes starve or fail to raise young, so they decline after the rabbit decline.

(c) Percent decrease = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100 = (600 − 1,500) ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **−60%**, a 60% decrease.

(d) It is a **negative feedback loop** (a rise in one population triggers changes that push it back down). Predation is **density-dependent**: the effect on prey depends on prey and predator density, and predators eat more when prey are plentiful.
:::

:::sol SG-U3-E016
**Answer:** a model experimental design follows.

(a) **Hypothesis:** If phosphorus is added to tanks of pond water, then the green alga population will grow larger (reach a higher population density) than in tanks with no added phosphorus.

(b) **Independent variable:** the amount of phosphorus added (phosphorus added or not). **Dependent variable:** the alga population (cell count per mL, or chlorophyll concentration, measured at regular intervals). **Controlled variables** (any two): light intensity and hours of light, temperature, volume and source of pond water, tank size and type, starting number of algae, same measuring times.

(c) **Treatment group:** 6 tanks receive the phosphorus solution (the same amount in each). **Control group:** 6 tanks receive an equal volume of water (or the solution without phosphorus) and are otherwise treated identically. Using six tanks per group gives replicates, so chance differences between tanks average out and the result is more reliable. Assign tanks to treatments at random so position near the light does not bias the result. Measure all tanks on the same schedule until the populations plateau.

(d) **Supporting results:** the phosphorus tanks reach a higher alga count (a higher growth rate or a higher plateau) than the control tanks, with the difference larger than the variation within a group. **Limitation:** small tanks in a lab are simpler than a whole pond, which has other nutrients (such as nitrogen), grazers, varying light and temperature, and water exchange, so the result might not scale to the pond. Other acceptable answers: a short study time, or other factors limiting growth in the pond.
:::

## Unit 2 — The Living World: Biodiversity

### Section A — MCQ Answers and Explanations

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ001
**Answer: D.** Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (gene versions) among the individuals of one population or species. A counts species (species diversity), B counts habitat types (habitat diversity), and C is simply population size, which says nothing about how varied the genes are.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ002
**Answer: A.** Absorbing storm surge and floodwater is a regulating service because the ecosystem moderates a natural hazard. Provisioning services supply goods such as food and timber, cultural services provide recreation and spiritual value, and supporting services (soil formation, nutrient cycling) underlie all the others.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ003
**Answer: C.** Primary succession begins where there is no soil, such as newly exposed bare rock. The abandoned hay field, burned forest, and logged hillside (A, B, D) all keep their soil and seed bank, so they undergo secondary succession.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ004
**Answer: B.** Ecological tolerance is the range of conditions, here temperature, that an organism can endure before injury or death. Carrying capacity (A) is the largest population an environment can sustain, biotic potential (C) is maximum reproductive rate, and species richness (D) is a count of species.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ005
**Answer: A.** Near islands receive more immigrants, and large islands have lower extinction rates because they hold bigger populations and more habitat. The combination of high immigration and low extinction gives the highest equilibrium richness. Option D is the opposite combination and would have the fewest species.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ006
**Answer: D.** A periodic process recurs at regular intervals, as a spring flood does. The meteorite impact, a single lightning-ignited fire, and an earthquake (A, B, C) are episodic or random events with no regular schedule.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ007
**Answer: B.** Natural selection acts on heritable variation: individuals whose traits improve survival and reproduction leave more offspring, so the trait becomes more common over generations. A describes inheritance of acquired traits, C wrongly claims need-driven change within one generation, and D is wrong because mutations arise randomly and are not directed by the environment.
:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ008

:::

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ009

:::sol QB-U2-MCQ010

### Section B — FRQ Solutions

:::sol QB-U2-FRQ001
(a) (i) Chloride concentration decreases as distance from the highway increases (about 620 mg/L at 10 m down to 40 mg/L at 400 m). (ii) The number of egg masses increases with distance (4 at 10 m up to 31 at 400 m). *(1 point: describes both trends correctly, including direction.)*

(b) The data are correlational. Distance from the highway also changes other factors (canopy cover, pool depth or size, vehicle pollutants, predators, noise), and any of these could affect egg-laying. Only a controlled experiment that changes chloride alone can show cause. *(1 point: identifies a confounding variable or states that correlation does not show causation.)*

(c) Hypothesis: if chloride concentration increases, then wood frog egg hatching success will decrease, because the eggs have a limited tolerance for salinity and high chloride causes osmotic stress that kills embryos above a threshold. *(1 point: testable prediction with direction; 1 point: justification linked to ecological tolerance.)*

(d) Independent variable: chloride concentration of the water. Dependent variable: hatching success (percent of eggs that hatch) or embryo survival. *(1 point for each.)*

(e) Set up at least five containers with chloride concentrations spanning the field range, for example 0 (control), 50, 150, 300, and 600 mg/L, with at least three replicate containers per concentration and the same number of eggs (e.g., 30) in each. Hold constant any two of: water temperature, water volume, pond-water source, light, egg-mass age or source, pH, or time. *(1 point: concentrations with a control and replicates; 1 point: two valid controlled variables.)*

(f) Plot percent hatching against chloride concentration. The tolerance range is the interval over which hatching stays high; the concentration at which hatching drops sharply marks the tolerance limit. Compare this limit with the chloride values measured in the pools. *(1 point: uses a hatching-versus-concentration pattern to identify the limit.)*

(g) Reduce road-salt use near vernal pools by applying less salt or using alternatives (sand, brine pre-wetting, or less harmful de-icers), or install vegetated buffers or swales that filter runoff. Tradeoff: reduced salt can mean less ice control and more accident risk, and alternatives or buffers cost more. *(1 point: valid solution with a stated tradeoff or cost.)*
:::

## 1.1 Geologic Time Scale
%% CED: none (academy extension)

:::box simple
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old. Geologists split that span into named blocks, as a historian splits history into ages. Squeeze all of Earth's history into one day and modern humans appear in the last few seconds.
:::

The **geologic time scale** is the calendar of Earth history. From longest to shortest its units are **eons, eras, periods** and **epochs**. The four eons are the Hadean, Archean and Proterozoic (together the Precambrian, nearly 90% of time) and the Phanerozoic ("visible life"). Boundaries fall where the fossil record changes sharply, and several mark **mass extinctions**, when a large share of species vanishes. Five major ones occurred, about 445, 370, 252, 201 and 66 million years ago (Ma).

![Figure 1.1 — The geologic time scale: the four eons (top) and the three eras of the last 541 million years (bottom)](figs/C1_geologic_time.png){w=0.7}

| Era (began) | Periods | Defining features |
|---|---|---|
| Paleozoic (541 Ma) | Cambrian to Permian | Marine animals diversify; fish, land plants, amphibians, reptiles. Ends with the Permian extinction (about 90% of marine species). |
| Mesozoic (252 Ma) | Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous | Dinosaurs, first mammals and birds. Ends with an asteroid impact. |
| Cenozoic (66 Ma) | Paleogene, Neogene, Quaternary | Mammals diversify; ice ages. The Holocene epoch began about 11,700 years ago. |

Life began in the oceans by about 3.5 billion years ago (Ga), and photosynthetic bacteria raised oxygen in the air about 2.4 Ga.

### Dating rocks

**Relative dating** orders events: in undisturbed layers (**strata**) older rock lies below younger rock, and a fault is younger than the rock it cuts. **Radiometric dating** gives ages in years. Unstable **parent isotopes** decay into **daughter isotopes** at a fixed rate called the **half-life**, the time for half of the atoms to decay. The clock starts when a mineral crystallizes, so igneous rock is dated directly and sedimentary layers are dated by igneous layers above and below. Carbon-14 (half-life 5,730 years) dates wood and bone up to about 50,000 years old; potassium-40 (1.25 billion years) and uranium-238 (4.47 billion years) date rocks.

:::box formula
**Fraction remaining = (½)ⁿ**, n = elapsed time ÷ half-life. **Age = n × half-life.**
100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% after 1, 2, 3 half-lives.
:::

:::box apply
Fossil fuels formed over millions of years and are burned in centuries, so they are non-renewable on human time scales, and a rapid CO₂ rise is a sharp break from the slow geologic record.
:::

:::box mistake
- Thinking a sample is "all decayed" after two half-lives. 25% remains.
- Using carbon-14 on very old rock; too little is left after about 50,000 years.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E001
A charcoal sample from an ancient hearth contains 12.5% of the carbon-14 found in living wood. The half-life of carbon-14 is 5,730 years. (a) Calculate how many half-lives have passed. (b) Calculate the age of the charcoal. (c) A volcanic rock contains 25% of its original potassium-40 (half-life 1.25 billion years); assume no loss of the daughter product matters. Calculate the rock's age in billion years. (d) Explain why carbon-14 cannot be used to date a 100-million-year-old fossil.
:::

:::box summary
- Earth is about 4.6 Ga old; time is divided into eons, eras, periods and epochs, and boundaries often mark mass extinctions.
- Radiometric dating uses half-lives: fraction remaining = (½)ⁿ.
:::

## 1.2 Earth's Structure
%% CED: none (academy extension)

:::box simple
Earth is like a peach: a thin skin (crust), thick flesh (mantle) and a dense stone (core of iron and nickel). Going down, everything gets hotter, denser and more squeezed.
:::

Early Earth was partly molten, so dense iron and nickel sank to the center and light rock rose (**differentiation**). Layers by composition:

- **Crust**: thin, solid. **Oceanic crust** is 5–10 km thick, dense basalt (about 3.0 g/cm³) and young. **Continental crust** is 30–70 km thick, lighter granite-type rock (about 2.7 g/cm³) and much older.
- **Mantle**: about 84% of Earth's volume, hot solid rock to 2,900 km depth that flows very slowly. Density 3.3–5.7 g/cm³.
- **Core**: iron and nickel, 10–13 g/cm³. The **outer core** is liquid and its motion makes Earth's magnetic field; the **inner core** is solid because of the enormous pressure.

By strength: the **lithosphere** (crust plus the rigid top of the mantle, about 100 km thick) is cool and brittle and is broken into **plates**. The **asthenosphere** below it is soft and flows slowly, so plates can move over it. Thick continents ride higher than ocean basins (**isostasy**).

![Figure 1.2 — Earth's layers by composition and by strength](figs/C1_earth_layers.png){w=0.75}

Nobody has drilled beyond about 12 km. The layers are mapped by **seismic waves**: they speed up or bend where material changes, and S waves cannot pass through liquid, so they vanish behind the liquid outer core.

:::box mistake
- Saying the mantle is liquid. It is flowing solid; only the outer core is liquid.
- Mixing up crust and lithosphere. The lithosphere includes the rigid top of the mantle.
:::

:::box summary
- Crust, mantle, core: density increases with depth; the outer core is the only liquid layer.
- The rigid lithosphere (in plates) floats on the soft asthenosphere.
:::

## 1.3 Plate Tectonics
%% CED: 4.1 | ERT-4.A.1, ERT-4.A.2, ERT-4.A.3, ERT-4.A.4, ERT-4.A.5 | LO: ERT-4.A

:::box simple
Earth's rigid shell is cracked into giant plates that creep about as fast as fingernails grow. Where plates push together, pull apart or slide past each other, the crust crumples, splits or snaps. Mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes cluster along those cracks.
:::

### The engine: moving plates

The lithosphere is broken into about a dozen large plates and many small ones. Heat from Earth's interior drives slow mantle convection, and the pull of cold, dense plate edges sinking into the mantle moves plates a few centimeters per year. **Plate motion is powered by Earth's internal heat, not by the Sun.** Evidence includes matching coastlines, rocks and fossils across oceans, seafloor that gets older with distance from a ridge, and GPS. Boundaries concentrate geologic activity, so a map of boundaries is also a map of risk.

### Three boundary types

At a **convergent boundary** plates move toward each other.

- *Oceanic meets continental.* The denser oceanic plate sinks in a **subduction zone**, forming a deep **trench**. Water squeezed out of the slab at about 100 km depth helps rock above it melt, so volcanoes lie roughly 100–200 km inland of the trench, not at it, and magma rises to build volcanoes and mountains (the Andes, the Cascades). Powerful earthquakes occur along the slab.
- *Oceanic meets oceanic.* One sinks, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, an **island arc** (Japan, the Aleutians). Oceanic plate cools and thickens as it ages away from a ridge, so the **older, cooler, denser plate** is the one that sinks.
- *Continental meets continental.* Neither sinks easily, so crust buckles into high mountains (the Himalayas). Earthquakes are common; volcanoes are rare.

At a **divergent boundary** plates move apart and magma rises to make new crust: a mid-ocean ridge with **seafloor spreading** under the sea, or a **rift valley** on land (East African Rift), with volcanoes and shallow earthquakes.

At a **transform boundary** plates slide past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed, but friction locks the plates and the result is earthquakes (the San Andreas Fault).

![Figure 1.3 — Convergent, divergent and transform plate boundaries](figs/C1_plate_boundaries.png){w=0.9}

### Hot spots and reading global maps

A **hot spot** is a plume of hot rock rising from deep in the mantle, far from a plate edge. The plate drifts over it, leaving a line of volcanoes: the active one over the plume, older extinct ones farther along the direction of plate motion (the Hawaiian Islands).

To read a boundary map: a long narrow line of volcanoes and earthquakes marks a boundary; volcanoes just inland from a trench mean oceanic–continental subduction; a curved island chain beside a trench means an island arc; earthquakes without volcanoes along a straight line mean a transform fault or continental collision; a lone volcano far from a boundary suggests a hot spot. The Pacific "Ring of Fire" has most of the world's earthquakes and active volcanoes because so many subduction zones lie there. Near a trench earthquakes are shallow, and farther **inland** they are progressively **deeper**, tracing the sinking slab; a graph of depth against distance from the trench that slopes down to the right signals subduction. Transform faults give only shallow earthquakes. Rifts and hot spots heat groundwater, giving hot springs and geothermal energy.

:::box formula
**Plate speed = distance between two volcanoes ÷ difference in their ages.** The active volcano has age 0.
Shortcut: 1 km per million years = 0.1 cm per year.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E011
Three extinct volcanic islands lie along a chain that ends at an active hot-spot volcano. Island P is 140 km from the active volcano and 2.0 million years old; Island Q is 350 km away and 5.0 million years old; Island R is 630 km away and 9.0 million years old. (a) Calculate the plate's speed from the data for Island R, in km per million years and in cm per year. (b) Check it with Island P. (c) Predict the age of an island 1,050 km from the active volcano. (d) State which direction the plate is moving relative to the hot spot and explain why the volcanoes are not found at a plate boundary.
:::

:::box mistake
- Saying plates "float on liquid magma." The asthenosphere is solid but flows slowly.
- Saying transform boundaries make volcanoes. They make earthquakes only.
- Saying all convergent boundaries make volcanoes. Continent collisions build mountains with few or none.
:::

:::box strategy
For a boundary question name the **motion** (toward, apart, sideways), the **landform** (trench, ridge, mountains) and the **hazard** (earthquake, volcano), using the CED words: island arcs, seafloor spreading, rift valleys, hot spots, faults.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E001
A geologist studies three sites. Site 1 is a line of active volcanoes 150 km inland, parallel to a deep ocean trench offshore. Site 2 is a long underwater mountain chain where new basalt is found and the seafloor gets older with distance on both sides. Site 3 is a straight valley where a road has been offset 4 m sideways by an earthquake, with no volcanoes nearby. (a) Identify the type of plate boundary at each site. (b) For Site 2, describe what happens to the plates and name the process that adds new crust. (c) State which site is most likely to produce a tsunami, and justify your choice in one sentence.
:::

:::box summary
- Plates move on the asthenosphere, driven by internal heat; boundaries concentrate quakes and volcanoes.
- Convergent: subduction, trenches, volcanic mountains, island arcs, or collision mountains. Divergent: ridges, rifts. Transform: earthquakes.
- Hot spots leave chains of volcanoes inside plates.
:::

## 1.4 Earthquakes
%% CED: 4.1 | ERT-4.A.3, ERT-4.A.5 | LO: ERT-4.A

:::box simple
Bend a ruler until it snaps: it stores energy, then releases it all at once. Rock along a fault does the same. Plates keep moving, the fault stays stuck, strain builds, and finally the rock slips and sends out shaking.
:::

:::box process
Plate motion → fault locks and stress builds → stress exceeds friction → sudden slip → stored energy released as seismic waves → shaking at the surface.
:::

An earthquake occurs when stress overcomes a locked fault, releasing stored energy. The underground point where slip begins is the **focus**; the surface point directly above is the **epicenter**. Smaller **aftershocks** follow. Most earthquakes occur at plate boundaries: transform faults, subduction zones (down to about 700 km) and ridges and rifts (shallow). Shallow quakes (under 70 km) do the most damage.

![Figure 1.4 — Focus and epicenter, and the wave arrivals on a seismogram](figs/C1_earthquake.png){w=0.85}

| Wave | Motion | Speed (crust) | Passes through |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P** | Push–pull | about 6 km/s (fastest) | Solids and liquids |
| **S** | Side-to-side | about 3.5 km/s | Solids only |
| **Surface** | Rolling along the ground | slowest | Surface; most damage |

A **seismograph** records P waves first, then S, then surface waves. The longer the S–P gap, the farther the station is from the quake; distances from three stations place the epicenter where three circles meet.

**Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source (one value per earthquake) on the logarithmic **moment magnitude** scale: each whole-number step is a tenfold larger wave amplitude and about 32 times more energy. **Intensity** describes shaking and damage at one place on the **Modified Mercalli** scale (I–XII); it falls with distance and rises on soft ground.

:::box formula
**Amplitude ratio = 10^ΔM; energy ratio ≈ 32^ΔM** (ΔM = magnitude difference, which may be a decimal; use a calculator).
**Distance = (S–P time) × vP·vS ÷ (vP − vS)**; for 6.0 and 3.5 km/s, 8.4 km per second of S–P time.
:::

Hazards: shaking, surface rupture, **liquefaction** (saturated sand and fill act like liquid and buildings tilt), landslides, fires, dam failure and tsunamis. Damage depends on magnitude, depth, distance, ground type and construction; soft sediment amplifies shaking.

:::box apply
Risk is cut by flexible reinforced building codes, retrofitting, keeping critical facilities off soft or filled ground, and drills. Early-warning systems detect fast P waves and alert people seconds before S and surface waves arrive. Earthquakes cannot be predicted, only forecast as probabilities.
:::

:::box mistake
- Confusing magnitude (energy at the source) with intensity (local effects).
- Putting the epicenter underground. The focus is underground; the epicenter is on the surface.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E002
A seismograph station records the first P wave at 08:14:10 and the first S wave at 08:14:35. Assume P waves travel at 6.0 km/s and S waves at 3.5 km/s. (a) Calculate the S–P interval in seconds. (b) Calculate the distance from the station to the earthquake. (c) Calculate how many seconds after the earthquake began the P wave arrived. (d) Explain why data from at least three stations are needed to locate the epicenter.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E003
Earthquake A has a moment magnitude of 6.1 and earthquake B has a magnitude of 8.1. (a) Calculate how many times larger the ground-motion amplitude of B is than that of A. (b) Using a factor of 32 per magnitude unit, calculate how many times more energy B releases. (c) State which measure, magnitude or Modified Mercalli intensity, can differ between two towns affected by the same earthquake, and explain why.
:::

:::box summary
- Stress builds on a locked fault; slip releases P, S and surface waves.
- Magnitude (logarithmic energy) differs from intensity (local effects). Most quakes occur at plate boundaries.
:::

## 1.5 Tsunamis
%% CED: 4.1 | ERT-4.A.5 | LO: ERT-4.A

:::box simple
A tsunami is a push through the whole depth of the ocean, not a wind wave. A seafloor jolt shoves a huge volume of water, and the push crosses the sea as a very long, fast, low wave that becomes huge only in shallow water.
:::

A **tsunami** is a series of very long ocean waves caused by sudden displacement of water. The usual cause is a large undersea earthquake at a subduction zone that lifts or drops the seafloor by meters; others are underwater landslides, volcanic eruptions and asteroid impacts. Sideways (transform) quakes seldom make large tsunamis.

![Figure 1.5 — A tsunami: seafloor displacement, a fast low wave in deep water, a slow tall wave near shore](figs/C1_tsunami.png){w=0.75}

The wavelength is about 100–200 km, far more than the ocean depth, so the wave's speed depends only on depth.

:::box formula
**v = √(g × d)**, g = 9.8 m/s², d = depth in m, v in m/s (× 3.6 for km/h). **Travel time = distance ÷ speed.**
At 4,000 m depth v ≈ 198 m/s (about 710 km/h).
:::

In the open ocean the wave is under 1 m high, so ships do not notice it. In shallow water it slows, its wavelength shortens and its energy is squeezed into less water, so height grows (**shoaling**) and water runs far inland (**run-up**). Several waves arrive, minutes to an hour apart, and the first is not always the largest; the sea may first drain away.

Tsunamis kill by drowning and debris, destroy ports and power plants (Fukushima, 2011) and wetlands and reefs, and salt water contaminates soil and freshwater aquifers. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (magnitude 9.1) killed over 200,000 people. Warning combines seismometers, deep-ocean pressure buoys (DART) and tide gauges. Across an ocean there are hours of warning; near a quake there are minutes, so strong shaking, a sudden sea retreat or a roar means *move to high ground or far inland now*. Mangroves and reefs reduce wave energy somewhat; zoning and drills help.

:::box mistake
- Calling a tsunami a "tidal wave." It has nothing to do with tides.
- Thinking it is tall at sea. It is low and long there and grows only in shallow water.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E004
A tsunami crosses an ocean 4,000 m deep. Use v = √(g × d) with g = 9.8 m/s². (a) Calculate the wave speed in m/s and in km/h. (b) Calculate the time, in hours, to reach a coast 2,500 km away, assuming constant depth. (c) Calculate the speed where the depth is 10 m. (d) Explain why the wave becomes taller as it approaches shore even though it slows down.
:::

:::box summary
- Undersea quakes at subduction zones (also landslides, eruptions) displace water and cause tsunamis.
- Speed = √(g·d): fast in deep water, slower and taller in shallow water. Sensors and evacuation plans save lives.
:::

## 1.6 Volcanoes
%% CED: 4.1 | ERT-4.A.1, ERT-4.A.2, ERT-4.A.4 | LO: ERT-4.A

:::box simple
Think of a shaken soda bottle. Thin, runny magma lets gas bubble out gently, like opening it slowly. Thick, sticky magma traps the gas until it blasts out, like soda spraying across the room.
:::

Rock melted inside Earth is **magma**; at the surface it is **lava**. Volcanoes form at **subduction zones** (water from the slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above; most explosive volcanoes), at **divergent boundaries** (pressure drops as plates part and mantle melts to basalt; Iceland) and at **hot spots** (Hawaii, Yellowstone).

Eruption style depends on the **silica (SiO₂) content** of the magma, which sets its **viscosity** (resistance to flow) and how much gas it traps.

| Magma | Silica | Temperature | Viscosity and gas | Eruption |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basaltic | about 50% | 1,000–1,200 °C | Low; gas escapes | Quiet lava flows |
| Andesitic | 57–63% | intermediate | Medium–high | Often explosive |
| Rhyolitic | over 68% | 650–800 °C | Very viscous; traps gas | Very explosive |

![Figure 1.6 — Three common volcano types](figs/C1_volcano_types.png){w=0.85}

A **caldera** is a large basin left when an emptied magma chamber collapses (Crater Lake). The **Volcanic Explosivity Index** (0–8) rises about tenfold per step with erupted volume.

Hazards: **lava flows** (usually slow); **pyroclastic flows**, glowing clouds of gas, ash and rock up to 700 °C moving over 100 km/h, the deadliest; **ash fall** that collapses roofs, grounds aircraft and harms lungs; **lahars**, volcanic mudflows down valleys; gases (SO₂ forms acid rain; CO₂ can pool and suffocate); and landslides and tsunamis.

**Climate.** A big explosive eruption puts SO₂ into the stratosphere, where it forms sulfate **aerosols** that reflect sunlight and cool Earth for one to three years (Pinatubo 1991, about −0.5 °C; Tambora 1815, "the year without a summer"). Volcanic CO₂ is far less than human emissions, so volcanoes do not explain modern warming. Volcanoes also give fertile ash soils, geothermal energy and new land. Scientists monitor swarms of small quakes, ground tilt, gas and heat, and authorities evacuate danger zones.

:::box mistake
- Saying all eruptions are explosive. Basaltic ones are usually gentle.
- Saying volcanoes warm the climate. A big eruption cools it for a few years.
:::

:::box strategy
Chain the logic: composition → viscosity → gas trapping → eruption style, then match volcano type to boundary or hot spot, hazards and a monitoring method.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E005
A town lies 18 km from a volcano's vent. A second town lies 45 km from the vent down a river valley. A pyroclastic flow moves at an average of 120 km/h and a lahar at an average of 36 km/h. (a) Calculate the time, in minutes, for the pyroclastic flow to reach the first town. (b) Calculate the time, in minutes, for the lahar to reach the second town. (c) State which hazard allows more time to evacuate and give one monitoring method that could provide warning. (d) Explain why rhyolitic magma tends to erupt more explosively than basaltic magma.
:::

:::box summary
- Volcanoes form at subduction zones, rifts and hot spots.
- High-silica, viscous, gas-rich magma erupts explosively; basalt flows quietly. Aerosols from big eruptions cool climate briefly.
:::

## 1.7 Solar Intensity, Seasons, and Latitude–Longitude
%% CED: 4.7 | ENG-2.A.1, ENG-2.A.2, ENG-2.A.3, ENG-2.A.4, ENG-2.A.5 | LO: ENG-2.A

:::box simple
Shine a flashlight straight down on a table, then tilt it. The straight beam makes a small bright spot; the slanted beam spreads the same light over a larger, dimmer area. Earth's curved surface does the same, so the equator is hot and the poles are cold.
:::

### Insolation, angle and latitude

**Insolation** (incoming solar radiation) is Earth's main energy source and depends on **season and latitude**. The **angle of the sun's rays** determines intensity. Because Earth is a sphere, the rays strike at 90° at only one latitude at a time, the place directly under the sun, where the energy is spread over the smallest area and passes through the least atmosphere. At higher latitudes rays arrive at a slant, spread over more area and cross more atmosphere, where more is absorbed, scattered or reflected. The highest radiation per unit area is at the equator and decreases toward the poles.

![Figure 1.7 — The same beam covers a small area at the equator and about twice the area at 60° latitude](figs/C1_insolation.png){w=0.65}

### The tilt makes the seasons

Earth's axis is tilted about **23.5°** and points in the same direction in space all year, so the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the sun for half the orbit and away for the other half.

- **June solstice (about June 21):** sun overhead at the Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N); Northern days longest, sun highest.
- **December solstice (about December 21):** sun overhead at 23.5° S; Northern days shortest, sun lowest.
- **Equinoxes (about March 20 and September 22):** sun overhead at the equator; day and night nearly equal everywhere.

![Figure 1.8 — The fixed tilt points the Northern Hemisphere toward the Sun in June and away in December](figs/C1_seasons.png){w=0.75}

The tilt also sets **day length**: about 12 hours all year at the equator, longer days in the summer hemisphere at higher latitudes, and 24 hours of daylight poleward of 66.5° in the summer hemisphere (north in June, south in December). A place receives the most radiation on its longest summer day and the least on its shortest winter day, because high sun angle and long daylight work together. On a day-length graph, a small swing means near the equator, a large swing a high latitude, and June maxima the Northern Hemisphere. With no tilt there would be no seasons.

:::box formula
**Noon sun angle = 90° − |latitude − latitude of the direct sun|**
Direct-sun latitude: +23.5° (June solstice), 0° (equinoxes), −23.5° (December solstice); north positive. Intensity on flat ground is proportional to the **sine** of the sun angle (the cosine of the angle from vertical): sin 60° ÷ sin 30° = 0.866 ÷ 0.500 ≈ 1.7.
**Energy absorbed = incoming sunlight × (1 − albedo)**; **albedo** is the fraction reflected (0–1). Extra energy absorbed when a bright surface is replaced by a dark one = incoming × (old albedo − new albedo), in W/m².
:::

### Latitude and longitude

**Latitude** is the angle north or south of the equator (0° to 90°); lines of equal latitude (**parallels**) run east–west. **Longitude** is the angle east or west of the **prime meridian** through Greenwich (0° to 180°); lines of equal longitude (**meridians**) run pole to pole. Give latitude first, with N/S and E/W. The **Tropics** (23.5° N and S) mark the farthest the sun can be overhead, and the **Arctic and Antarctic Circles** (66.5°) mark where 24-hour day or night occurs, giving tropical (0–23.5°), temperate (23.5–66.5°) and polar (66.5–90°) zones.

![Figure 1.9 — Latitude and longitude; the point is 40° N, 75° W](figs/C1_latlong.png){w=0.75}

:::box formula
**1° of latitude ≈ 111 km. 1° of longitude ≈ 111 km × cos(latitude).**
Earth turns 15° per hour, so places 15° apart in longitude differ by 1 hour of solar time; the one farther east is ahead.
:::

:::box mistake
- Saying seasons occur because Earth is closer to the sun in summer. Earth is closest in early January; tilt, not distance, causes seasons.
- Saying the tilt changes during the year. Only Earth's position in orbit changes.
:::

:::box strategy
Give **two** linked reasons for seasonal change, sun angle (intensity per area) and day length (hours of exposure), and trace both to the tilt.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E016
A city is at 52° N. (a) Calculate the noon sun angle on the June and December solstices. (b) Calculate how many times more intense the noon sunlight is in June than in December, using the sine of each angle. (c) A snow-covered field with albedo 0.80 receives 180 W/m² and then melts to bare soil with albedo 0.15. Calculate the increase in energy absorbed per square meter. (d) Explain why a city at 5° N has much smaller seasonal temperature changes than this city.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E008
A city lies at 40° N. (a) Calculate the noon sun angle at the city on the June solstice and on the December solstice using the formula in this guide. (b) Identify which date gives more intense radiation per unit area at the city, and explain why with reference to area and atmospheric path length. (c) State a second reason, other than sun angle, that the city receives more total energy on the June solstice than on the December solstice. (d) At which latitude is the sun directly overhead on the March equinox?
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E006
Two research stations are both at 52° N. Station 1 is at 100° W and Station 2 is at 70° W. Use 1° of latitude = 111 km and 15° of longitude = 1 hour of solar time. (a) Calculate the difference in solar time between the stations and state which is ahead. (b) Calculate the distance between the stations along the 52° N parallel, using cos 52° = 0.616. (c) Calculate the distance from Station 1 north to the Arctic Circle (66.5° N) along its meridian. (d) Explain why 1° of longitude is a shorter distance at 52° N than at the equator.
:::

:::box summary
- Intensity depends on the sun's angle and is greatest at the equator; Earth's 23.5° tilt, not distance, causes seasons and day length.
- Latitude (north–south) and longitude (east–west) locate places; 1° latitude ≈ 111 km.
:::

## 1.8 Soil
%% CED: 4.2, 4.3 | ERT-4.B.1, ERT-4.B.2, ERT-4.B.3, ERT-4.C.1, ERT-4.C.2, ERT-4.C.3, ERT-4.C.4 | LO: ERT-4.B, ERT-4.C

:::box simple
Soil is crushed rock mixed with dead plant and animal matter, air, water and living things. A centimeter takes hundreds of years to form, but one storm can wash it away. Soil also filters water like a coffee filter. Coarse sand drains like gravel; fine clay holds water like a hard-to-wring sponge; loam, a balanced mix, gives the best of both.
:::

### How soil forms

Soil begins as **parent material**, bedrock or loose sediment, broken down by weathering. **Physical weathering** (freeze–thaw, root wedging, temperature swings, abrasion) breaks rock without changing its chemistry. **Chemical weathering** (weak acids, oxidation, hydrolysis) changes minerals. Lichens, roots, burrowers and microbes add to both and supply organic matter. Weathered material may stay put or be **transported** by water, wind, ice or gravity and **deposited** elsewhere (floodplains, windblown loess, glacial till). Soil depends on parent material, climate, organisms, slope and time. Warmth and moderate rain speed weathering and decomposition; cold or dryness slow them. On new ground such as cooled lava, **primary succession** begins as lichens and mosses release acids and leave organic remains. **Humus**, the dark, stable end product of decomposition, gives fertile soil its crumbly structure and nutrient-holding ability.

### Soil horizons

Soil develops layers called **horizons**; a vertical cut through them is a **soil profile**.

![Figure 1.10 — A generalized soil profile with its horizons](figs/U4_soil_horizons.png){w=0.6}

Percolating water carries dissolved minerals and fine clay out of upper layers (**leaching**) into the **B horizon**. The **A horizon** is topsoil, the most biologically active layer, with **biotic** parts (roots, earthworms, fungi, bacteria) and **abiotic** parts (sand, silt, clay, minerals, water, air). O-horizon litter shields soil from raindrops, holds moisture and recycles nutrients, so losing it dries and erodes the soil. Young and dry-climate soils may lack an E horizon.

### Erosion and soil protection

**Erosion** moves soil: wind lifts loose, dry, bare soil (the 1930s Dust Bowl); water erodes by raindrop splash, **sheet** erosion, **rills** and **gullies**. It is worst on bare, steep, loose soil, and topsoil, with its nutrients and humus, goes first. Eroded sediment and attached fertilizer, pesticides and bacteria cloud streams, smother fish eggs and fuel algal blooms. Continuous cropping removes plant matter and speeds decomposition, so soil organic matter (humus) falls unless residue, manure or cover crops return it. Intact soil does the opposite: it traps sediment, clay and humus hold pollutants and nutrient ions, and microbes break down contaminants. **Protecting soils protects water quality.** Soluble nitrate is not held tightly, so excess fertilizer leaches to groundwater. Conservation methods keep cover (cover crops, perennials), use no-till, plow along contours, terrace, strip-crop, plant windbreaks and leave vegetated buffer strips; each slows wind or water, binds soil with roots or covers the surface.

:::box formula
**Net soil change = formation rate − erosion rate** (e.g., t/ha/yr). **Total = net rate × area (ha) × time (yr).**
**Depth lost = mass lost ÷ (bulk density × area)**; 1 ha = 10⁴ m²; × 1,000 for mm. Loss is sustainable only if depth lost per year does not exceed depth formed per year.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E012
A 25-ha field loses soil at 12 t/ha/yr while new soil forms at 1.0 t/ha/yr. The soil's bulk density is 1.25 t/m³. (a) Calculate the net loss per hectare per year. (b) Calculate the total net loss from the whole field over 30 years. (c) Calculate the depth of soil lost per year, in mm, from the erosion rate. (d) New soil forms at about 0.08 mm per year. Justify whether the erosion is sustainable, and propose one practice to bring it closer to the formation rate.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E002
A soil pit shows these layers from the top down. P (0–3 cm): loose, dark, partly decayed leaves. Q (3–15 cm): dark brown, crumbly, many roots, rich in humus. R (15–28 cm): pale gray, sandy, few nutrients. S (28–60 cm): reddish brown, sticky, clay-rich. T (60–110 cm): angular broken rock pieces with a little clay. U (below 110 cm): solid, unbroken rock. (a) Match each layer to its horizon letter (O, A, E, B, C or R). (b) Explain why layer R is pale and nutrient-poor while layer S is clay-rich. (c) Identify the layer that a farmer loses first when heavy rain erodes a bare field, and describe one practice that would reduce this loss.
:::

### Sand, silt and clay; porosity and permeability

**Sand** (0.05–2.0 mm) is gritty, **silt** (0.002–0.05 mm) smooth like flour, **clay** (under 0.002 mm) sticky and ribbon-forming. **Texture** is their proportion. **Porosity** is the percent of a soil's volume that is pore space; **permeability** is how quickly water moves through; clay can be highly porous yet have low permeability because its pores are tiny. **Water-holding capacity** is the total water a soil holds against gravity; it varies with soil type, and water retention supports productivity and fertility. Particle size and composition of each horizon affect porosity, permeability and fertility; for example, a sandy A horizon over a clay B horizon drains quickly then perches water above the clay. **Fertility** depends on N, P, K, pH, organic matter and soil life.

| Property | Sandy | Silty | Clay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pore size | Large | Medium | Very small |
| Permeability | High | Medium | Low |
| Water-holding capacity | Low | Medium–high | High |
| Nutrient retention | Poor (leaches) | Moderate | Good |
| Problems | Droughty | Easily eroded | Waterlogged, hard to till |

### The soil texture triangle

A **soil texture triangle** plots clay, silt and sand percentages (summing to 100%) and names the soil. Find clay on the left scale and follow its horizontal line; find sand on the bottom scale and follow its line up and left; the lines cross in the soil's class (silt is a check). Two components suffice. **Loam**, a blend of clay, silt and sand with a specific range, not equal thirds, supports a wide variety of crops because it drains yet holds water and nutrients.

![Figure 1.11 — The soil texture triangle; the circled point is 35% clay, 25% silt, 40% sand (clay loam)](figs/U4_gap_texture_triangle.png){w=0.8}

| Group | Drainage | Water and nutrient holding |
|---|---|---|
| Sandy (sand, loamy sand, sandy loam) | Fast | Low to moderate |
| Loamy (loam, silt loam, silt) | Moderate | Moderate to high |
| Clay-loamy (clay loam, sandy clay loam, silty clay loam) | Moderately slow | High |
| Clayey (sandy clay, silty clay, clay) | Very slow | Highest |

A **landfill liner** needs clay-rich soil, whose tiny pores block leachate; compost helps a sandy garden hold water. For worked examples we also use a simplified key:

| Class | Rule (percent by mass) |
|---|---|
| Sand | sand ≥ 85 |
| Clay | clay ≥ 40 |
| Silt | silt ≥ 80 |
| Loam | sand 23–52, silt 28–50, clay 7–27 |

Any mixture fitting no row is "intermediate texture."

### Testing soil

- **Physical:** the **jar test** (sand settles in about a minute, silt in hours, clay in a day or more; readings are cumulative, so layer thickness is the difference between readings; percent = layer ÷ total sediment × 100), sieving, and a **percolation test** timing drainage.
- **Chemical:** pH, N–P–K kits, salinity (electrical conductivity).
- **Biological:** organic matter, earthworm counts, soil respiration (CO₂ release).

Results guide decisions: lighter, more frequent irrigation for sand; targeted phosphorus rather than blanket fertilizer; lime for low pH; drainage or raised beds for waterlogged clay. **Infiltration rate** = depth of water ÷ time (mm/h).

:::box formula
**Percent by mass** = (component mass ÷ total mass) × 100. **Water retained** = poured − drained; **percent retained** = retained ÷ poured × 100.
**Runoff rate = rainfall rate − infiltration rate** (zero if rain is slower than infiltration).
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E014
A ring is filled with 30 mm of water on Field A and on Field B. The water soaks in completely in 45 minutes on A and in 6 hours on B. (a) Calculate the infiltration rate of each field in mm/h. (b) A storm drops 12 mm of rain per hour for 3 hours. Calculate the runoff depth from each field during the storm, assuming the rates stay constant. (c) Identify which field is more likely to be compacted clay and state one effect on a nearby stream.
:::

For an investigation, include a **control** (the untreated or bare comparison, such as bare soil beside grass) and name the **independent variable** (what you change, e.g. soil type), the **dependent variable** (what you measure, e.g. drained volume after 10 minutes), the **controlled variables** (same soil mass, water volume, container, time) and **replicates** (at least three, averaged), and write an if–then hypothesis.

:::box mistake
- Confusing weathering (breaks rock in place) with erosion (moves material).
- Calling the whole soil "topsoil." Topsoil is the A horizon (with the O above it).
- Mixing up porosity and permeability, or reading the triangle with one percentage.
:::

:::box strategy
For a protection method give **how it works** (slows water, binds soil, covers surface) and **what it protects** (topsoil, nutrients, water quality). In data questions quote numbers to justify which soil holds the most water or drains fastest.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E003
A technician oven-dries and sieves a 50.0 g soil sample and obtains 21.0 g of sand, 18.0 g of silt and 11.0 g of clay. (a) Calculate the percent by mass of sand, silt and clay. (b) Using the simplified key in this guide, name the texture class. (c) State which of the three components is most responsible for holding water against gravity, and explain why in terms of pore size.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-U4-E004
A student tests three soils (X, Y, Z). For each, she places 400 g of dry soil in an identical funnel lined with filter paper, pours in 200 mL of water at once, and records the volume that has drained into a cylinder after 10 minutes. Results: soil X, 170 mL; soil Y, 110 mL; soil Z, 40 mL. (a) Calculate the percent of the poured water retained by each soil. (b) Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable and two controlled variables. (c) One soil is a sand, one a loam and one a clay. Assign each, and justify using the drained volumes. (d) Propose one improvement that would make her conclusion more reliable.
:::

:::box summary
- Soil forms as parent material is weathered, transported and deposited; horizons (O, A, E, B, C, R) differ in composition and organic content.
- Wind and water erode soil; conservation keeps cover and slows flow; healthy soil filters water.
- Texture (sand–silt–clay, read on the triangle) controls porosity, permeability and water-holding; loam is the balanced blend.
- Physical, chemical and biological tests guide irrigation and fertilizer decisions.
:::

## 1.9 The Rock Cycle
%% CED: none (academy extension)

:::box simple
Rocks are slowly recycled. A mountain wears down to sand, sand is buried and cemented into stone, stone is baked or squeezed into a new rock, and deep enough it melts and may erupt again. Earth's heat and the Sun's energy keep the cycle turning.
:::

Rocks are made of **minerals**, natural solids with a definite chemistry and crystal structure.

| Family | How it forms | Clues | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Igneous** | Magma or lava cools and crystallizes: *intrusive* (slow, underground, large crystals) or *extrusive* (fast, at the surface, tiny crystals or glass) | Interlocking crystals, no layers | Granite; basalt, obsidian |
| **Sedimentary** | Sediment is compacted and cemented (**lithification**); some form from minerals precipitated from water or from shell and plant remains | Layers, rounded grains, fossils | Sandstone, shale; limestone, coal |
| **Metamorphic** | Existing rock changes in the solid state under high heat and pressure; minerals recrystallize or align in bands (*foliation*) | Banded, hard, shiny | Slate, gneiss; marble, quartzite |

![Figure 1.12 — The rock cycle](figs/C1_rock_cycle.png){w=0.8}

Processes: **weathering and erosion** break rock into **sediment** (the parent material of soil) and **deposition** lays it in layers; **compaction and cementation** make sedimentary rock; **metamorphism** (heat and pressure, no melting) makes metamorphic rock; **melting** makes magma; **cooling and crystallization** make igneous rock. The cycle has many routes: igneous rock can be metamorphosed without weathering, and any rock can be weathered again. Plate tectonics drives it: subduction drags rock down to melt, collisions metamorphose rock, volcanoes make new igneous rock and uplift exposes rock to weathering, over thousands to hundreds of millions of years.

:::box apply
Granite and sandstone are building stone; limestone is crushed for cement and lime; coal is a fuel; many metal ores form where hot fluids move through igneous rock; marble and slate are metamorphic building stones.
:::

:::box mistake
- Believing the cycle runs in one fixed order. Any rock can become any type, and steps can be skipped.
- Saying metamorphic rock melted. It changed in the solid state; if it melts it becomes magma.
:::

:::box example
id: SG-C1-E007
Sediment accumulates on a shallow sea floor at an average of 0.40 mm per year. When it becomes sedimentary rock, compaction reduces its thickness by 40%. (a) Calculate the thickness of sediment, in meters, deposited in 5.0 million years. (b) Calculate the thickness of rock formed after compaction. (c) Name the process that turns loose sediment into rock. (d) The rock is limestone, which is later buried deep and heated and squeezed without melting. Name the rock family and one example of the rock that forms.
:::

:::box summary
- Igneous rock cools from melt; sedimentary rock forms from cemented sediment; metamorphic rock is changed by heat and pressure.
- Weathering, deposition, lithification, metamorphism, melting and crystallization link them; plate tectonics drives the cycle.
:::

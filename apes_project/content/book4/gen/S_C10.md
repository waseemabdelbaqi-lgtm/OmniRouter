### Section B — FRQ Solutions

:::sol CB-C10-FRQ001 | label=Question 1 | year=2005
**(a)** Pick, e.g., SARS (new) and malaria (old). SARS: virus spread by respiratory droplets and close contact; control by quarantine, masks, handwashing. Malaria: parasite spread by infected mosquito bites; control with nets, repellent, drained standing water, spraying or biological control. *(1 transmission + 1 control per disease = 4)*

**(b)** For malaria: warmer or wetter climate expands mosquito breeding range, or habitat change creates standing water, raising infections. *(factor 1, causal explanation 1)*

**(c)** Diseases spread quickly because travel and trade move infected people, animals and vectors worldwide; deforestation, crowding and climate change bring humans into contact with new pathogens. *(mechanism 1, matching explanation 1)*

**(d)** HIV/AIDS: spread through unprotected sex, shared needles, blood and mother-to-child; no cure or vaccine and long latency. (Influenza/pneumonia in an aging population also accepted.) *(1 disease, 1 cause)*
:::

:::sol CB-C10-FRQ002 | label=Question 2 | year=2010
**(a)** Choose one pollutant and stay with it for all three sub-parts.
- (i) *Source (1 pt).* Any specific non-plant source: mercury from coal burning, dental fillings or broken fluorescent bulbs; lead from old paint, solder, plumbing or leaded ceramics; PCBs from old transformers and capacitors.
- (ii) *Entry route (1 pt) and one health effect (1 pt).* Example for mercury: eating contaminated fish (it biomagnifies as methylmercury) or breathing vapor; effect: nervous-system damage and developmental delay. Lead: swallowing paint chips or dust; effect: lowered IQ and learning problems. PCBs: eating contaminated fatty fish; effect: immune and developmental problems, possible cancer.
- (iii) *Two control steps, neither a ban (2 pts).* For example: public education on exposure, safe collection and disposal programs, scrubbers or filters on emission sources, treatment of water supplies, cleanup or capping of contaminated soil and sediment, substituting safer materials. Each must be a concrete action that lowers exposure.

**(b)** *(1 pt)* Children take in more air, water and food per kilogram of body mass than adults, and their organs and nervous systems are still developing, so the same exposure does more damage. (Hand-to-mouth behavior or a weaker detoxifying liver are also accepted.)

**(c)** Cost = 40 kg CO₂ × $0.75/kg CO₂ = **$30**. *(1 pt setup with units cancelling, 1 pt answer.)*

**(d)** *(1 pt)* Pick one pollutant and match a real control: SOₓ → flue-gas scrubber or removing sulfur before burning; NOₓ → selective catalytic reduction or lower-temperature combustion; particulates → electrostatic precipitator or baghouse filter. Fluidized-bed combustion or coal gasification are also accepted.

**(e)** *(2 pts, one per reason, each discussed)* Typical reasons: weaker or less strictly enforced pollution and workplace rules lower compliance cost; lower wages and benefits; cheaper land, raw materials and taxes or subsidies; access to a large local market. Name the reason and say why it saves the company money.
:::

:::sol CB-C10-FRQ003 | label=Question 3 | year=2002
Data (100 shrimp per dish): ≤0.0001% → 10 dead; 0.001% → 10; 0.01% → 20; 0.1% → 55; 1% → 90; 10% → 100.

**(a)** Put concentration on the logarithmic x-axis and number dead on the linear y-axis; plot the six points and draw a smooth S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. *(1 point axes/points, 1 point smooth curve)*

**(b)** LD50 is the dose that kills half of the test population. Reading the curve at 50 dead gives a concentration just below 0.1%, about 0.07–0.08% (interpolating on the log scale between 0.01% and 0.1%). Mark it on the graph. *(1 point definition, 1 point value read from the graph; "about 0.1%" with no marking was judged too vague)*

**(c)** The threshold is the highest dose that produces no response above the background effect. The 10% mortality at the lowest two concentrations is background, so response first rises above it between 0.001% and 0.01%; the threshold is about 0.001%. *(1 point definition, 1 point value)*

**(d)** For: both are animals with similar basic physiology, the dose–response pattern probably holds, testing on people is unethical so animal tests give estimates. Against: humans differ in size, metabolism and exposure route (swallowed or touched, not immersed in water), a short 48-hour test on an invertebrate cannot capture chronic effects, and species vary in sensitivity. *(2 points for, 2 for against; maximum 4)*
:::

:::sol CB-C10-FRQ004 | label=Question 4 | year=2004
**(a)** Source: coal burning in power plants. Mercury leaves with the flue gas, travels on wind, and returns by rain or dry fallout onto land and water; microbes convert it to methylmercury that enters food webs. *(source 1, transport 1, entry to water 1; up to 3)*

**(b)** Reduce emissions by fuel switching or efficiency (lower-mercury coal, natural gas, wind/solar, conservation), by washing coal before burning, or by post-combustion capture (activated-carbon sorbent with filters, scrubbers). Describe how each cuts mercury. *(1 point each)*

**(c)** Mercury biomagnifies: it is stored in tissue and not excreted, so concentrations increase at each trophic level; big predators eat many contaminated prey and live long, bioaccumulating more over a lifetime. *(up to 3 points)*

**(d)** Lead: from mining and smelting, old paint, leaded gasoline, plumbing, batteries. Acute sublethal effects: abdominal pain, headaches, anemia, and nervous-system effects such as learning problems or irritability. Cadmium, arsenic also accepted. *(1 point each: metal, release route, effect)*
:::
